Overview

A group of naturally fibrous silicate minerals historically used in technical textiles. Included only as historical material context.

Feel and behavior

Historical heat-resistant mineral fiber.

Where you find it

Historical insulation, fire-resistant cloth and industrial products.

Source and geography

Asbestos is not one mineral but a commercial and regulatory term for naturally fibrous silicate minerals. They fall into two groups: serpentine, represented by chrysotile, and amphiboles, including crocidolite, amosite, tremolite, anthophyllite and actinolite. Deposits have been mined on every continent except Antarctica. Chrysotile has made up about 95 percent of asbestos used worldwide in recent decades, and about 2 million tons a year were still being used in the mid-2010s, notably in India, China and Russia. This atlas includes asbestos only as historical and safety context and does not recommend handling or processing it.

Advantages

  • Historically it resisted heat and fire, which drove its use in protective and insulating products.
  • It could be spun and woven like a textile fiber.
  • It resisted many chemicals and was cheap, which explains its once-wide adoption.
  • These historical properties do not justify any current use.

Drawbacks

  • IARC classifies all forms of asbestos as carcinogenic to humans (Group 1).
  • Inhaled fibers cause asbestosis, lung cancer and mesothelioma, often decades after exposure.
  • Legacy material in buildings, vehicles and products still exposes workers and occupants when disturbed.
  • Removal and disposal are costly and must follow strict regulations.

Worth knowing

  • The EPA's 2024 chrysotile rule was the first ban finalized under the agency's revised TSCA section 6 authority.
  • Among the last US uses of chrysotile were diaphragms in chlor-alkali plants that make chlorine and caustic soda.
  • In June 2025 EPA told the Fifth Circuit it planned to reconsider parts of its chrysotile rule, then withdrew that request the following month.

Types, grades and fabrics

Types and grades

Asbestos minerals are classified for regulation, not for purchase. Regulators distinguish chrysotile, the serpentine form behind most historical use, from the amphiboles crocidolite, amosite, tremolite, anthophyllite and actinolite. Historically, the long fibers suited to spinning were graded for textiles, while shorter grades went into cement sheet, floor tile, friction products and insulation. In the United States, the EPA's 2024 rule addressed the last known ongoing uses of chrysotile: diaphragms in chlor-alkali plants, sheet gaskets, oilfield brake blocks, aftermarket automotive brakes and linings, and other vehicle friction products. This entry describes these categories only for historical and regulatory context.

Fabrics and products

Historical asbestos textiles included woven cloth, tape, rope, yarn, lagging and felt, used in fire blankets, heat-resistant gloves and aprons, theater safety curtains, boiler and stove door rope seals, pipe and duct wrap, electrical insulation and laboratory equipment. Asbestos was often blended with a carrier fiber such as cotton to make it spinnable. Such items can survive in old buildings, ships, workshops, laboratories and collections, which is why museums and property owners treat unknown vintage heat-resistant textiles with caution.

Buying and care

How to judge quality

This atlas gives no buying, selling or handling advice for asbestos. Asbestos is banned or restricted in many countries; the International Ban Asbestos Secretariat lists national bans in roughly 70 countries, with 2025 totals varying by source and by how partial bans are counted. Legacy items are the realistic concern: old fire blankets, gloves, rope gaskets, lagging and lab mats that are gray-white, stiff or felt-like may contain asbestos, but appearance cannot confirm it. If an item is suspected, do not buy, sell, use or disturb it; only an accredited laboratory sample taken by a qualified professional can establish its content.

Care in detail

Do not disturb. Suspected asbestos textiles or materials should not be cut, brushed, washed, vacuumed, repaired, moved, bagged or thrown out by an untrained person. Keep people away from damaged material and leave assessment, sampling, removal and disposal to licensed asbestos professionals working under the rules of the jurisdiction, such as EPA and OSHA requirements in the United States or national rules implementing EU directives. Museums and owners of historic collections should consult conservators and licensed assessors. This is general safety information, not legal or professional advice.

Care at a glance

Use the manufacturer’s application-specific maintenance and handling instructions. Technical performance cannot be inferred from household laundry guidance.

Environmental and social footprint

Asbestos's footprint is measured in disease. Beyond the annual death toll estimates in the research profile, chrysotile is still mined and used in some countries while dozens of others have banned it. In the United States, the EPA published its Part 1 chrysotile rule under TSCA section 6 on 28 March 2024, effective 28 May 2024, banning the remaining known uses with phased compliance dates. Industry groups challenged it in the Fifth Circuit; in 2025 EPA sought time to reconsider parts of the rule, then withdrew that motion in July 2025, leaving the rule in place while litigation continued. Legacy asbestos in buildings and waste remains the main exposure route.

Tradeoffs

Evaluate the specified grade, whole product, useful lifetime and realistic recovery route. A technical property does not establish a lower overall impact.

A useful question to ask: What exact material, product form and documented producer stand behind this name?

Science

Inorganic material

Composition, filament size and product form determine properties and handling.

Structure and chemistry

Chrysotile is a magnesium sheet silicate whose layers curl into hollow, scroll-like fibrils, so its fibers are curly and comparatively soluble in lung fluid. Amphiboles are double-chain silicates that form straight, needle-like, highly biopersistent fibers. Both split lengthwise into ever finer fibrils. Disease potency depends on mineral type, fiber length, width and biopersistence: one review links pleural disease to biopersistent fibers thinner than about 0.1 micrometers and longer than about 5 micrometers. Nonfibrous forms of the same minerals exist, and fibrous amphiboles such as winchite and richterite, not historically regulated as asbestos, contaminated vermiculite.

From source to yarn

This atlas does not recommend handling, processing or disturbing asbestos in any form; what follows is historical description only. Mined ore was crushed and milled to free the fibers, which were graded by length, with the longer grades going to textiles. Fibers were carded, often with a carrier fiber such as cotton, spun and woven into cloth, tape, rope and lagging for insulation, gaskets, brake linings and fire-resistant clothing. Textile work was intensely dusty: at a South Carolina plant that processed chrysotile from Quebec, lung cancer risk per unit exposure was about 50 times that in Quebec mining and milling.

Performance in use

Asbestos was valued for resistance to heat and chemical breakdown, tensile strength and the ability to be spun and woven, which earned it the label 'miracle mineral'. Those properties do not justify its use: exposure causes asbestosis, lung cancer and malignant mesothelioma, with latencies of up to about 50 years for mesothelioma. Amphiboles are more potent for mesothelioma than chrysotile, but all forms are carcinogenic. Legacy material persists: asbestos-containing brake pads were still found in Hungarian waste streams twenty years after the EU ban. Suspected asbestos textiles, such as old heat-resistant cloth, gloves, rope or gaskets, should be left undisturbed and assessed by qualified professionals.

How it is identified

Do not burn, cut, tease apart or sample suspected asbestos. Identification belongs to accredited laboratories. Phase contrast microscopy of air samples counts fibers longer than 5 micrometers, thicker than about 0.25 micrometers and with an aspect ratio of at least 3:1, but cannot tell asbestos from other fibers. Electron microscopy with elemental analysis identifies mineral type and thin fibers, and the EU is moving occupational measurement from optical to electron microscopy by the end of 2029.

History

Timeline

  1. 1924W. E. Cooke reported fibrosis of the lungs due to inhalation of asbestos dust in the British Medical Journal, an early clinical description of asbestosis. [1]
  2. 1955Richard Doll reported excess lung cancer mortality among asbestos workers. [2]
  3. 1960Wagner, Sleggs and Marchand described 33 cases of diffuse pleural mesothelioma in South Africa, all but one with probable exposure to crocidolite. [3]
  4. 1964Selikoff, Churg and Hammond linked asbestos exposure among insulation workers to lung cancer, mesothelioma and other neoplasms. [4]
  5. 1989EPA issued the Asbestos Ban and Phase-out Rule under TSCA in July; in 1991 the Fifth Circuit vacated most of it in Corrosion Proof Fittings v. EPA, 947 F.2d 1201. [5]
  6. 1999Commission Directive 1999/77/EC required EU member states to ban the use of chrysotile asbestos by 1 January 2005, with a limited exception for existing electrolysis diaphragms. [6]
  7. 2023Directive (EU) 2023/2668 lowered the EU occupational exposure limit to 2,000 fibres/m3, counting fibres 0.2 to 3 micrometers wide. [7]
  8. 2024EPA published its Part 1 chrysotile asbestos rule under TSCA section 6 on 28 March, banning the remaining known ongoing uses with phased compliance dates. [8]

Asbestos was once woven into heat-resistant products. Its serious inhalation hazards transformed how legacy materials are managed; suspected material should not be disturbed and needs qualified assessment.

The deeper record

Asbestos has been used since antiquity, but at industrial scale only for roughly the last 100 to 150 years. Asbestosis was the first disease identified, and asbestos-caused cancer was recognized in the late 1930s, yet world consumption approached 5 million tons a year in the 1990s. Controversies over the relative hazards of fiber types and sizes, some described by researchers as manufactured, slowed bans. A 2018 estimate put deaths caused by asbestos at about 255,000 a year, about 233,000 of them from work exposure, borne by miners, textile workers, insulators and builders. Legacy asbestos in buildings, vehicles and waste keeps the hazard current.

Labeling and law

Asbestos is regulated as a hazardous substance, not merely labeled as a fiber. The European Union banned asbestos in 2005, and Directive (EU) 2023/2668 lowered the occupational exposure limit from 100,000 to 2,000 fibres per cubic meter when fibres 0.2 to 3 micrometers wide are counted, or 10,000 when thinner fibres are included. In the United States, the EPA issued a ruling in 2024 to ban chrysotile asbestos. More than 50 countries had banned asbestos by the mid-2010s. IARC classifies all forms of asbestos as carcinogenic to humans (Group 1). This is not legal advice.

Frequently asked

Is asbestos banned in the United States?

The EPA's 2024 TSCA rule bans the remaining known uses of chrysotile asbestos, with phased deadlines, and was in effect as of the sources reviewed, though litigation continued. Older asbestos in buildings is not removed by the rule and is managed under separate abatement and workplace regulations.

Is asbestos banned worldwide?

No. Many countries, including all EU member states, have national bans, and the International Ban Asbestos Secretariat's list counted roughly 70 countries in 2025, with totals varying by source. Chrysotile is still mined and used elsewhere, so bans remain uneven.

How can I tell if an old fire blanket contains asbestos?

You cannot tell by look or feel. Only an accredited laboratory analyzing a sample taken by a qualified professional can confirm it. Leave the item undisturbed, keep people away if it is damaged, and contact a licensed asbestos assessor.

Is chrysotile safer than other asbestos?

Chrysotile is less biopersistent than amphiboles and less potent for mesothelioma, but IARC classifies all asbestos forms, chrysotile included, as carcinogenic to humans. Regulators treat it as a serious hazard, which is why the EPA's 2024 rule targeted it.
