Overview

A term for yarn spun from dog undercoat, usually in small-scale craft contexts.

Feel and behavior

Variable by coat; often warm with a halo.

Where you find it

Keepsake yarn, hand knits and blends.

Source and geography

Chiengora is a coined craft term, combining the French chien (dog) with angora, for yarn spun from the undercoat of the domestic dog (Canis familiaris). Fiber is usually collected by brushing shedding double-coated dogs and spun by hand spinners or very small mills, often as keepsake yarn from an owner's own pet. There is no industrial supply chain; quantities per dog are small and seasonal, and fiber quality varies strongly with breed and with how well guard hair is excluded during collection.

Advantages

  • Uses fiber that would otherwise be discarded after grooming.
  • Medullated undercoat gives a warm, lofty, haloed yarn.
  • Keepsake yarn has personal value that commercial fiber cannot offer.
  • No animal is harmed when fiber is brushed out during natural shedding.

Drawbacks

  • Yarn sheds and can develop a wet-dog smell if not thoroughly washed.
  • Dog allergens are present in hair, and so-called hypoallergenic breeds are no exception.
  • Quality varies strongly by breed and collection, and quantities per dog are small.
  • Commercial sale of dog-hair products is legally uncertain in the US and banned in the EU for dog fur.

Worth knowing

  • A 2012 study found higher Can f 1 allergen levels in the hair and coats of so-called hypoallergenic dog breeds than in Labrador retrievers.
  • The medulla that makes dog hair lofty also makes the fiber less resistant to bending, one reason chiengora sheds.
  • In a five-breed study, only the finer Lhasa Apso and Pomeranian hair was slender enough for machine spinning.

Types, grades and fabrics

Types and grades

Chiengora has no formal grades; spinners sort it by breed, coat type and how cleanly the undercoat was separated. Double-coated breeds with long, dense undercoats, such as Samoyed, Siberian Husky, Great Pyrenees and Newfoundland, give the most spinnable fiber, while short, stiff single coats are generally unsuitable. An academic comparison of five breeds found similar chemistry but large differences in diameter and linear density, with only Lhasa Apso and Pomeranian hair fine enough for machine spinning. Trade forms are raw brushings, washed and carded batts, and finished yarn, which is either 100 percent dog undercoat or a blend with wool or silk.

Fabrics and products

Most chiengora is hand spun into knitting or crochet yarn and made into scarves, hats, mittens and shawls, often as keepsakes from a household pet. Blends with wool add elasticity and reduce shedding; silk adds strength and sheen. Plied yarns are preferred because they shed less than singles. Woven items are uncommon, though some weavers use dog-hair yarn as weft in blankets. The historical parallel, Coast Salish blankets woven with woolly-dog fiber, belongs to a distinct Indigenous tradition and should not be confused with modern pet yarn.

Buying and care

How to judge quality

Good chiengora starts with clean, guard-hair-free undercoat brushed during shedding; stiff, straight hairs in the yarn signal poor sorting and will prickle. Wash quality matters: well-scoured yarn smells neutral even when damp. Plied, moderately twisted yarn sheds less than loosely spun singles. Because processing is small-scale, ask a mill or spinner about minimum fiber weight, blend options and whether batches are kept separate if you want your own dog's fiber back. Commercial sale of dog-hair products in the US raises unresolved questions under the Dog and Cat Protection Act, so most chiengora is custom spun rather than sold at retail.

Care in detail

Hand wash in lukewarm water, about 30 C, with a wool or pet-safe mild detergent, and rinse thoroughly, since residual skin oils cause odor. A vinegar rinse is sometimes used by spinners to reduce smell. Do not agitate or change water temperature abruptly, as the fiber can felt, especially in wool blends. Press out water in a towel and dry flat, away from heat. Do not tumble dry or iron directly; steam lightly if needed. Store clean and dry with a moth deterrent, because dog hair is keratin and attracts clothes moths and carpet beetles. Expect some shedding; plied yarns shed less.

Care at a glance

Follow the complete product’s care label. Blends, dyes, finishes and construction can change suitable washing, drying and storage.

Environmental and social footprint

Chiengora is a waste-recovery craft: fiber comes from routine grooming of companion animals and needs no land, feed or animals raised for the purpose. Environmental inputs are limited to washing water, detergent and the energy of small-scale carding and spinning, and no life cycle data exist. Animal welfare concerns are minimal when fiber is brushed out during shedding. The main social issue is legal and ethical: the US Dog and Cat Protection Act and the EU ban on cat and dog fur address dog fur products, and how they apply to yarn from shed hair is not settled, so commercial sellers should get legal advice.

Tradeoffs

Origin, processing inputs, labor, product construction and useful lifetime all need specific evidence. The name alone does not establish responsible production.

A useful question to ask: What exact material, product form and documented producer stand behind this name?

Protein-based natural material

Most hair is keratin; silk is a different protein-fiber system.

Structure and chemistry

Dog coats combine guard hairs with a finer undercoat. A study characterizing hair from five breeds (Labrador, Golden Retriever, German Shepherd, Pomeranian and Lhasa Apso) found chemical, morphological and thermal properties broadly similar across breeds, but diameter and linear density differed markedly, and only the finer Lhasa Apso and Pomeranian hairs had the slenderness needed for machine spinning. Dog hair is keratin with a scaled cuticle, and many fibers contain a medulla described as fragmented or interrupted. This air-filled core lowers density and adds insulation but reduces flexibility.

From source to yarn

Undercoat is collected by brushing during shedding, kept as free as possible of guard hair, burrs and dander, and washed gently in lukewarm water with mild detergent to remove skin oils and reduce odor. Because the undercoat is short, slippery and has little crimp, spinners usually card it together with a carrier fiber such as wool to give the yarn cohesion, then spin it loosely to preserve loft and halo. Small batches make consistent grist and color difficult, so each lot tends to be unique. Plying adds strength and reduces shedding.

Performance in use

Dog undercoat yarn is warm and lofty, which craft sources attribute to the medullated fibers; a widely repeated claim that it is up to 80 percent warmer than wool is not tied to a standard test and should be treated as anecdotal. One academic comparison reported a tenacity of about 2.14 grams per denier, higher than the other animal fibers it tested, though samples were small. The yarn sheds, can felt, and may carry odor when damp if not thoroughly washed. It dyes with acid dyes like other keratin fibers and is susceptible to moths.

How it is identified

Burn testing (indicative only; take precautions) shows ordinary hair behavior. Microscopy can suggest canid hair from scale pattern and medulla form, but separating domestic dog from other canids, or from some other furs, requires reference collections or DNA analysis. This matters because Congress, in enacting the Dog and Cat Protection Act, found that dog and cat fur products were often deceptively labeled so that buyers could not tell what fur they contained.

Spinning canine hair has occurred in distinct craft traditions. Modern pet-undercoat yarn should not be conflated with the specific histories of Indigenous woolly-dog textiles.

The deeper record

The best-documented historical dog-wool tradition is that of Coast Salish peoples of the Pacific Northwest, who bred and managed long-haired woolly dogs over millennia and wove their fiber into blankets and clothing. A 2023 study in Science combining ancient genomics with Coast Salish knowledge analyzed the pelt of Mutton, a woolly dog who died in 1859 and whose pelt the naturalist George Gibbs sent to the Smithsonian. Mutton had about 85 percent precolonial woolly dog ancestry, the lineage diverged from other dogs up to about 5,000 years ago, and candidate genes for the woolly coat were identified. The weaving tradition declined in the nineteenth century and the dogs were lost.

Labeling and law

The Dog and Cat Protection Act of 2000 (19 U.S.C. 1308) makes it unlawful to import, export, or sell, advertise or distribute in interstate commerce any dog or cat fur product, meaning merchandise made in whole or in part of dog fur or cat fur. The statute defines dog fur as the pelt or skin of any animal of the species Canis familiaris. Whether yarn from shed hair detached from any skin falls within that definition is not settled by the text alone; this atlas does not resolve it. Civil penalties can reach 10,000 dollars per violation. The EU bans cat and dog fur products under Regulation (EC) No 1523/2007.

This describes what rules and standards cover. It is not legal advice; jurisdiction, product form and current rule text control.

Frequently asked

Can I make yarn from my dog's hair?

Yes, if your dog has a soft, reasonably long undercoat. Brush during shedding, keep the undercoat separate from guard hairs and debris, and store it dry. Several ounces are usually needed for a small project, and many spinners blend it with wool.

Will chiengora smell like dog?

Properly washed yarn should not. Odor comes from skin oils and dander left in the fiber, so thorough scouring before spinning and a careful wash of the finished item are important. A lingering smell when damp suggests incomplete cleaning.

Is dog hair yarn safe for people with dog allergies?

Not necessarily. A 2012 study found the major dog allergen Can f 1 in hair and coat samples of all breeds tested, with higher levels in so-called hypoallergenic breeds. Washing may reduce allergen, but it has not been shown to remove it.

Is dog hair yarn warmer than wool?

Craft sources often say so, attributing warmth to the air-filled medulla of dog hair, but no standardized test supports a specific figure. Warmth in a finished item depends mostly on yarn thickness, spinning style and knit structure.
