Overview

A trade name associated with red-deer down from New Zealand. Supplier-specific specifications require verification.

Feel and behavior

Marketed as a fine down; this atlas does not independently validate performance claims.

Where you find it

Specialty luxury knitwear and accessories.

Source and geography

Cervelt is a trade name associated in this atlas with the downy undercoat of farmed red deer, Cervus elaphus, in New Zealand. The ownership, current producers and volumes behind the name could not be verified from primary sources. What is documented is the biology: red deer grow a winter coat of coarse guard hairs and fine underhairs, and the underhair was characterized in detail in 2010 by researchers at AgResearch in Lincoln, New Zealand.

Advantages

  • The producer reports fineness comparable to fine cashmere.
  • Its producer says the fiber's wavy curl spins into a strong, resilient yarn.
  • Blends with merino or cashmere can extend warmth and softness into lighter garments.
  • Published microscopy shows red deer underhair is structurally close to fine wool.

Drawbacks

  • Very limited supply makes it costly and hard to buy or verify.
  • Performance claims come mainly from the producer and sellers.
  • It is often blended, so the Cervelt share of a garment may be small.
  • Like other fine animal fibers, it pills, felts and attracts moths.

Worth knowing

  • According to its producer, only about 20 grams of pure Cervelt can be collected from a single deer.
  • A New Zealand newspaper reported socks made with Cervelt selling for $1,700 a pair.

Types, grades and fabrics

Types and grades

Cervelt is a single proprietary fiber rather than a family of grades. Its website says it was developed by Douglas Creek Ltd, a New Zealand company, over about eight years, and describes it as the down of the New Zealand red deer, Cervus elaphus. The producer states a diameter of about 13 microns and that only about 20 grams of pure fiber can be collected per deer. These are producer claims and were not independently verified here. It is sold as dehaired fiber and as yarn, for example by New Zealand yarn and knitwear businesses, and in blends. Retail listings sometimes pair the name with other fibers, such as 'yangir cervelt', so the fiber content line matters more than the name.

Fabrics and products

Cervelt is used for small quantities of luxury knitwear and accessories: sweaters, stoles, scarves, beanies and socks, sometimes as 100% Cervelt and often blended with merino, cashmere or silk to extend supply and add strength. New Zealand knitwear makers sell Cervelt garments under their own labels, and a New Zealand newspaper reported Cervelt socks priced at $1,700 a pair. Yarn for hand knitting is sold in small skeins. Because output is limited, woven cloth is rare. The producer says the fiber has a wavy curl that spins into a resilient yarn and absorbs and releases moisture readily, which would suit next-to-skin knits, but these are marketing claims.

Buying and care

How to judge quality

Buyers should ask for the fiber share, since blends are common, and for any laboratory report of mean diameter and species. Real fine down feels soft, warm and slightly springy; a harsh or slick hand suggests a different or treated fiber. Check knit density and seams as for cashmere: loose, lofty knits pill faster. Because deer is not a named specialty fiber under the US Wool Products Labeling Act, a US label would normally say deer hair or similar, and in the EU it falls under animal hair; a label reading only 'Cervelt' is incomplete. Extreme prices are expected, so a low price is a warning sign. Provenance can be checked with the maker and with DNA or protein testing laboratories.

Care in detail

Treat Cervelt like fine cashmere unless the maker says otherwise. Hand wash in cool water, about 20 to 30 C, with a wool or cashmere detergent, without rubbing or wringing; rinse at the same temperature. Roll in a towel to remove water and dry flat, away from sunlight and radiators. Do not tumble dry. Steam lightly or iron on the wool setting (two dots, about 150 C) through a damp cloth if needed. Remove pills with a cashmere comb and store folded, clean, in breathable bags with regular checks for clothes moth and carpet beetle larvae. Blends with silk or merino follow the more delicate fiber's rules.

Care at a glance

Use the maker’s instructions rather than assuming standard wool care applies.

Environmental and social footprint

Cervelt's environmental and welfare profile cannot be independently assessed from public sources. It derives from New Zealand's farmed red deer industry, which raises deer mainly for venison and velvet antler, so the fiber would be a minor co-product of a pastoral system with its own land use and emissions. How the down is collected, whether by combing, shearing or from slaughtered animals, is not publicly documented in detail, and no life cycle assessment or welfare audit specific to Cervelt was found. At an estimated 20 grams per animal according to the producer, total volumes are tiny. A buyer interested in impact should ask the maker for collection methods, farm standards and any third-party audits.

Structure and chemistry

Transmission and scanning electron microscopy of red deer winter coat show that all fiber types have overlapping cuticle cells around a cortex, often with a central medulla, but differ in diameter, cuticle thickness, scale pattern and the proportion of medulla. Fine underhairs resemble fine sheep wool: their intermediate filaments are arranged as in wool orthocortex and paracortex cells, with a similar bilateral distribution of the two cell types. Guard hairs and antler velvet hairs are more variable and resemble the heterotype cortex cells described in human hair.

From source to yarn

Red deer grow their winter coat under photoperiodic control: longer nightly melatonin secretion as days shorten depresses prolactin, and earlier onset of winter coat growth is associated with lower prolactin. In a British trial, melatonin implants advanced winter coat growth by 35 days, while lactation combined with restricted food delayed it by 20 days. Harvest would need to coincide with the molt of the winter coat and be followed by dehairing, scouring and spinning; the producer's actual collection and processing steps for Cervelt could not be verified.

Performance in use

No independent test data for Cervelt fabrics were found. Because the fine underhair has a wool-like bilateral cortex, it would be expected to behave like fine wool or cashmere in moisture uptake, flame behavior (charring and self-extinguishing) and acid dyeing, and to share their susceptibility to pilling, felting and attack by clothes moth and carpet beetle larvae. Any claim of greater warmth or softness than cashmere is a producer claim to be checked against test reports.

How it is identified

Burn tests (indicative only, and to be done with care) show keratin behavior only. Deer guard hairs are recognizable microscopically by their scale pattern and prominent medulla, but fine underhair looks wool-like, so microscopy alone may not confirm deer origin in a blend. DNA or peptide mass spectrometry against authenticated red deer reference fiber would be needed to verify a Cervelt claim.

The deeper record

Commercial history for the Cervelt name, including dates, companies and volumes, could not be verified, so none is given. The scientific record is clearer. Work on red deer photoperiod, melatonin and prolactin in the 1990s explained the timing of winter coat growth, and the 2010 ultrastructural study from AgResearch showed that red deer underhair is structurally close to fine wool. That is the kind of evidence a producer would need to support textile use, but it does not describe any specific commercial product.

Labeling and law

Cervelt is a trade name, not a generic fiber name. Deer is not among the specialty fibers of the US Wool Products Labeling Act, so under 16 CFR 303.6 the content would be disclosed with the animal's name and the word hair, fiber or blend when present at 5% or more, for example deer hair, or as fur fiber. In the EU, deer hair falls in the animal hair category of Annex I of Regulation (EU) No 1007/2011, and the trade name cannot replace that disclosure.

This describes what rules and standards cover. It is not legal advice; jurisdiction, product form and current rule text control.

Frequently asked

What is Cervelt made from?

Cervelt is a trade name for the fine winter undercoat, or down, of farmed New Zealand red deer. According to its producer, it was developed by Douglas Creek Ltd, measures about 13 microns and yields only about 20 grams per deer. These are producer statements; independent test data are limited.

Is Cervelt softer than cashmere?

The producer claims it is lighter and softer than cashmere, and its stated 13 micron diameter is within the fine cashmere range. Independent comparative tests were not found. Softness also depends on spinning, knitting and finishing, so compare actual garments rather than fiber names.

Why is Cervelt so expensive?

Supply is very small: the producer says only about 20 grams of pure fiber can be collected per deer, and dehairing fine down from coarse deer hair is labor intensive. Luxury knitwear makers sell it in small batches. A newspaper report of $1,700 socks illustrates the price level.

How should Cervelt be labeled?

Cervelt is a trade name, not a generic fiber name. In the US a label would normally identify the animal, for example deer hair, with percentages; in the EU deer fiber falls under animal hair. The trade name can appear alongside, not instead of, that disclosure.
