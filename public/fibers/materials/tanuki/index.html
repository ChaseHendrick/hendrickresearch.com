Overview

Fur from a raccoon dog, sometimes sold under the name tanuki. It is not raccoon fiber.

Feel and behavior

A long-haired fur surface used as a pelt or trim.

Where you find it

Trim and fur products.

Source and geography

The raccoon dog, Nyctereutes procyonoides, is an East Asian canid, not a raccoon (Procyon); tanuki is its Japanese name, and in European fur farming it is known as Finnraccoon. Chinese geneticists describe the Chinese raccoon dog, N. p. procyonoides, as one of the most important fur-bearing species farmed in China, where it is raised alongside foxes and mink, for example in Hebei, Jilin and Xinjiang. Farmed stock includes the wild type and at least three coat color variants.

Advantages

  • Long, voluminous guard hair gives a full, lofty trim at low weight.
  • It insulates well around hoods and collars.
  • It dyes readily in many colors.
  • It has usually been cheaper than fox or mink.

Drawbacks

  • Species confusion and mislabeling are documented, including sale under other animal names.
  • Welfare concerns in cage farming were highlighted by EFSA in 2025.
  • The species is listed as an invasive alien species of Union concern in the EU, which restricts keeping and breeding.
  • Long hair tangles and sheds, and dressed skins need specialist care.

Worth knowing

  • The EU listed the raccoon dog as an invasive alien species of Union concern in 2019, with a transition period linked to requests to continue fur farming.
  • Congressional reports on the 2010 Truth in Fur Labeling Act described raccoon dog fur being sold as other animals or unlabeled because of a $150 exemption.
  • Despite its name and masked face, the raccoon dog is a canid, more closely related to foxes than to raccoons.

Types, grades and fabrics

Types and grades

Raccoon dog fur is sold under several names, which is itself the main buyer issue: tanuki (Japanese), Finnraccoon (Nordic farmed), and, in older US trade usage, Asiatic raccoon. Pelts come from farms in China and Finland and, less commonly, from wild animals. Farmed stock includes a wild-type grizzled gray-brown and several color mutations. Pelts are graded by size, density, guard hair length and color, and are frequently dyed or tipped to imitate other furs, or sheared. Because the raccoon dog is a canid, it is a different animal from the North American raccoon, Procyon lotor, and trade names should never replace the species name.

Fabrics and products

Raccoon dog fur is used above all as trim: hood ruffs on parkas and down jackets, collars, cuffs, and pompoms on hats, boots, bags and key chains. Its long, shaggy pile gives volume at low weight. Full coats, vests and stoles exist but are less common than trim. Dyed raccoon dog is used in bright fashion colors, including in low-cost accessories. Faux fur in acrylic or modacrylic imitates it for the same uses. Because trims and pompoms are small, they are easy to sell without clear species information, which is why labeling rules matter most here.

Buying and care

How to judge quality

Check first that the item is labeled and names the species correctly. Parting the hair to find skin rather than a knitted backing confirms real fur; a faux pompom has a fabric base and hair that melts when singed. Good pelts have long, even guard hairs over dense underfur and supple leather without cracks or odor. In the US, the Fur Products Labeling Act requires the animal name and country of origin, and the Truth in Fur Labeling Act of 2010 removed an exemption that had let items with less than $150 of fur go unlabeled; the legislative record cites raccoon dog fur sold under other animal names. A cheap pompom described as faux but with a skin base is mislabeled.

Care in detail

Care follows general fur practice; the label and a furrier govern. Detach trims from parkas before washing the garment. Store items on padded hangers or flat in breathable cotton bags, never sealed plastic, in a cool, dark and ventilated place, and inspect them for moth and carpet beetle larvae. If the fur gets wet, shake it and let it dry at room temperature, away from heat, then shake again to restore the loft; never use a hair dryer or iron. Keep perfume and hairspray away. Have soiled pieces cleaned by a fur specialist rather than washing them. Pompoms can be fluffed by gently shaking; brushing tears long guard hairs.

Care at a glance

Use item-specific specialist care and avoid heat or improvised cleaning.

Environmental and social footprint

Raccoon dog fur combines farm welfare issues with ecological and public health concerns. EFSA's 2025 opinion identified locomotory disorders and isolation stress among welfare consequences for caged raccoon dogs. Introduced populations have spread widely in Europe, and the EU added it to its list of invasive alien species of Union concern under Regulation (EU) No 1143/2014 through Implementing Regulation (EU) 2019/1262, with a transition period that allowed member states to seek authorization for continued farming. Mixed-species farms have also recorded viral infections in raccoon dogs. Processing adds dressing and dyeing chemistry, and independent life cycle data are lacking.

Science

Species or trade-specific material

Public specifications and independently verified sourcing can be limited.

Structure and chemistry

Raccoon dog pelts carry long, coarse guard hairs over a thick underfur, giving a shaggy, voluminous pile used mainly for trim. The hairs are keratin, with cuticle, cortex and a medulla in the guard hairs, and coat color depends on the amount and distribution of eumelanin and pheomelanin. Researchers ruled out alternative splicing and coding changes in the agouti signaling protein gene as the cause of the farmed color variants, so their genetic basis remains open. As with other furs, the skin must be dressed.

From source to yarn

Raccoon dogs are farmed in cages, pelted after the winter coat develops, and their skins dried, dressed and sometimes dyed before use as trim. Disease surveys report canine distemper virus, porcine circovirus 4 and Cryptosporidium in farmed raccoon dogs, reflecting the risks of dense and sometimes mixed-species husbandry; one porcine circovirus 4 detection came from a farm keeping pigs and raccoon dogs together. Experimental infection showed that raccoon dogs are susceptible to SARS-CoV-2 and transmit it to in-contact animals without showing illness.

Performance in use

The dense underfur and long guard hairs insulate well and give volume, but long hair tangles and breaks with abrasion, and dressed skins are sensitive to water and heat, so specialist cleaning is advised. Keratin hair is vulnerable to clothes moth and carpet beetle larvae in storage. In a laboratory study of pelts that included Finn raccoon, UV light failed to inactivate SARS-CoV-2, while heating at 60 C for one hour did.

How it is identified

Burn tests (indicative only, and to be done with care) separate real hair from melting acrylic or modacrylic faux fur but cannot identify species. Because raccoon dog is easily confused by name with raccoon and by appearance with other long-haired furs, identification relies on guard hair microscopy against reference hairs and on DNA. Polymorphic microsatellite markers developed for the Chinese raccoon dog support individual identification and cross-amplify in Arctic and silver foxes.

The deeper record

Raccoon dogs are farmed for fur in Europe, where the Finnraccoon name is used, and in China, and the species became prominent in coronavirus research. Raccoon dogs were considered possible intermediate hosts of the SARS coronavirus in 2002 to 2004, and genetic analysis of environmental samples from the Huanan market in Wuhan found raccoon dog DNA in SARS-CoV-2-positive samples from a wildlife stall, placing the species on a shortlist of potential intermediate hosts. In 2025 EFSA identified locomotory disorders and isolation stress as species-specific welfare consequences for farmed raccoon dogs, alongside the movement and exploration limits shared by all caged fur species.

Labeling and law

In the US, fur apparel falls under the Fur Products Labeling Act, whose FTC rules at 16 CFR Part 301 govern how the animal name and other information appear on labels, invoices and advertising. A trade name such as tanuki or Finnraccoon should not substitute for accurate species identification, and raccoon dog is not raccoon. In the EU, Regulation (EU) No 1007/2011 requires textile products that contain non-textile parts of animal origin, such as fur trim, to state this on the label.

Frequently asked

Is tanuki fur raccoon fur?

No. Tanuki is the Japanese name for the raccoon dog, Nyctereutes procyonoides, a member of the dog family. The North American raccoon, Procyon lotor, is an unrelated animal. US labels should use the correct animal name, and raccoon dog should not be described as raccoon.

How can I tell if a 'faux fur' pompom is real?

Part the hair at the base. Real fur is attached to leather skin; faux fur is attached to a knitted or woven fabric. You can also pull a hair or two and singe them carefully: real hair smells like burnt hair and curls to ash, while acrylic melts into a bead.

Is it legal to farm raccoon dogs in Europe?

The raccoon dog is on the EU list of invasive alien species of Union concern, which generally prohibits keeping and breeding. A transition period allowed member states to seek authorization for continued farming. National fur farming bans also apply in several countries, so rules depend on the country and date.

What is Finnraccoon?

Finnraccoon is a trade name for raccoon dog fur from farms in Finland. It refers to the same species, Nyctereutes procyonoides, as tanuki. Trade names should appear alongside, not instead of, the species name.
