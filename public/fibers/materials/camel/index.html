Overview

Fine down from the camel coat, separated from coarse hair; Bactrian camel is a familiar source.

Feel and behavior

Soft warmth and a characteristic natural tan palette.

Where you find it

Coats, knitwear, scarves and blankets.

Source and geography

Camel hair in textiles is associated above all with the two-humped Bactrian camel, Camelus bactrianus, of the cold deserts of Central and East Asia, and fiber studies also cover the one-humped dromedary, C. dromedarius. Old World camels were domesticated roughly 3,000 to 6,000 years ago and still supply milk, meat, draught and wool; in Kazakhstan, for example, camels are raised as meat, dairy and wool animals. The wild two-humped camel, Camelus ferus, is a separate, critically endangered species whose lineage diverged from domestic Bactrians around a million years ago.

Advantages

  • Down gives light, warm coats and knits.
  • Natural tan to brown shades need no dye.
  • Down is gathered at the seasonal molt from animals kept for milk, meat, transport and wool.
  • Camel hair coating drapes softly and takes a fine brushed finish.

Drawbacks

  • Soft, brushed surfaces flatten and wear at cuffs and pockets.
  • Residual guard hair can prickle and show as dark specks.
  • Pale shades need bleaching because the fiber is naturally brown.
  • Color names cause confusion, since 'camel' coats are often wool or synthetic.

Worth knowing

  • Camelids, including Bactrian camels, emitted less methane per kilogram of body mass than roughage-fed ruminants in a 2014 study, but about the same per unit of digestible fiber eaten.
  • Bactrian camel guard hairs can be several times thicker than the down, which is why dehairing matters so much for soft cloth.

Types, grades and fabrics

Types and grades

Camel fiber is sorted into down and hair. Dehaired Bactrian camel down is commonly reported at about 16 to 20 microns, intermediate hairs at about 20 to 29 microns and guard hairs from about 30 microns up to well over 100. Baby camel hair, from young animals, is the finest grade, reported at about 16 to 17 microns. Down is collected as the coat sheds, then sorted by color, from light tan to dark brown, and by fineness. Production figures vary widely because some sources include hair and down together. Bactrian fiber comes from Central and East Asian herds, and Inner Mongolia has been a major source within China.

Fabrics and products

Dehaired camel down is woven into classic camel hair coating, often a soft twill or double cloth with a brushed surface, and into jackets, blazers and blankets. It is also knitted into sweaters, scarves and hats, often in its natural tan. Blends with wool lower cost and add durability in coats, and blends with cashmere or silk add softness or luster. Coarse camel hair goes into interlinings, rugs and ropes.

Buying and care

How to judge quality

The word camel on a label may be a color, so read the fiber content line for 'camel hair' and its percentage. Good camel hair coating feels soft, light and dense, with a fine, even nap and few stiff, dark guard hairs poking through. Baby camel hair is softer and costs more. In blends, a small camel share mainly adds color and hand. Brushed surfaces should be even rather than patchy. Consider weight and tightness of weave for coats, since a dense cloth resists abrasion and wind better than a loose, lofty one.

Care in detail

Camel hair coats and tailored jackets are usually dry clean only; follow the label. Brush coats regularly with a soft clothes brush in the direction of the nap to remove dust, and air them between wears. Hang coats on shaped hangers. For knits, hand wash in cool water with a wool detergent free of enzymes and bleach if the label allows, and dry flat. Steam out creases or press through a cloth at the two-dot setting (up to 150 C), lifting the iron to avoid crushing the nap. Store clean, covered in breathable garment bags, and protect against moth larvae.

Care at a glance

Follow the label, brush coats gently and give garments time to air between wears.

Environmental and social footprint

Camel hair is a co-product of herding for milk, meat and transport, so fiber-specific life cycle data are scarce and allocation choices would dominate any result. A 2014 respiration chamber study that included Bactrian camels, alpacas and llamas found that camelids produced about 0.32 liters of methane per kilogram of body mass per day, versus about 0.58 in literature values for roughage-fed ruminants, with no significant difference once expressed per unit of digestible fiber eaten; lower emissions reflected lower relative food intake. Collection at the molt avoids shearing stress, although practices vary. Social impacts depend on the price herders receive for raw fiber and on dehairing, which is often done industrially rather than by herders.

Tradeoffs

Collection, dehairing and blending affect quality. The color name camel does not guarantee camel-fiber content.

A useful question to ask: Does the composition actually include camel hair, and in what proportion?

Protein-based natural material

Most hair is keratin; silk is a different protein-fiber system.

Structure and chemistry

Camel coat is a double coat of keratin fibers: coarse, often medullated outer hair over a fine, soft undercoat. In a year-long follicle study of Iranian dromedaries, mean fiber diameter was about 19 microns, about 18% of fibers were medullated, clean yield was about 78%, and the ratio of secondary to primary follicles averaged about 6.9. In Sudanese camels, fiber diameter correlated strongly with prickle factor (r = 0.83) and with the share of medullated fibers (r = 0.73), so the coarse fraction drives discomfort. The natural tan to brown color comes from melanin pigment.

From source to yarn

The coat grows and sheds seasonally; in dromedaries, follicle activity fell to a minimum in February and recovered in spring. Loosened undercoat is gathered by hand, combed or shorn around the molt, then sorted by color and fineness and scoured. Dehairing, on machinery of the kind used for cashmere, removes coarse outer hair, which is used separately for coarser products. The dehaired undercoat is carded and spun on woollen or worsted systems, often left undyed to use its natural color or blended with wool for coating fabrics.

Performance in use

Dehaired camel undercoat is light, warm and soft, with the hygroscopic, self-extinguishing behavior of keratin and an affinity for acid dyes, though its natural color limits pale shades without bleaching. Because it is fine and often lightly twisted, it abrades and pills more readily than tightly spun worsted wool, and brushed coat surfaces flatten with wear. Residual coarse, medullated hair raises prickle. Like other animal fibers it is attacked by clothes moth and carpet beetle larvae and weakened by alkaline washing.

How it is identified

Burn tests (indicative only, and to be done with care) show keratin behavior. Microscopy of diameter, scale pattern and medullation is the usual first step, and because camel-colored cloth is common, a label claim should be checked by fiber analysis rather than color. MALDI-TOF peptide profiling and ATR-FTIR with chemometrics have both distinguished camel from cashmere, wool, mohair, yak, alpaca and other animal fibers. Hybrid camels bred from dromedary and Bactrian parents complicate species claims, and diagnostic SNP panels can detect hybrid ancestry in live animals.

Camel hair has long served pastoral textile needs, with fine down and coarse hair used differently. Industrial coating fabrics made the material widely recognizable, although camel-colored cloth is not necessarily made from camel hair.

The deeper record

Camels carried people and goods along cross-continental caravan routes and supplied wool as well as milk, meat and draught; archaeological dating places two-humped camel domestication about 5,000 to 6,000 years ago, and genetic work shows the living wild camel is a separate lineage rather than the direct ancestor of domestic Bactrians. Hybrids between dromedaries and Bactrians, once valued as strong pack animals, are now bred in the Middle East and Central Asia partly to improve milk or wool quality. Conservation geneticists stress keeping domestic herds diverse and protecting the last wild two-humped camels in Mongolia and China.

Labeling and law

The US Wool Products Labeling Act of 1939 includes camel hair among the specialty fibers within the definition of wool, and FTC rules exclude it from the catch-all term fur fiber, so a product may state camel hair with its percentage. In the EU, Annex I of Regulation (EU) No 1007/2011 lists camel among the named fine animal hairs, followed or not by wool or hair. The color word camel is not a fiber name: a camel-colored coat made of wool or polyester must still carry its actual fiber content.

This describes what rules and standards cover. It is not legal advice; jurisdiction, product form and current rule text control.

Frequently asked

Is a camel coat made from camel hair?

Not always. Camel is also a color name, and many camel-colored coats are wool, cashmere or synthetic. Only the fiber content line tells you. In the US and EU, camel hair must be stated with its percentage when present.

What is baby camel hair?

Baby camel hair is fiber from young camels, the finest grade, reported at about 16 to 17 microns. It is softer than adult down and is used in premium coats, scarves and knitwear, often in natural light tan.

How do I keep a camel hair coat looking good?

Brush it with a soft brush after wearing, hang it on a broad, shaped hanger, let it air between wears and dry clean it only when needed. Steam out creases rather than pressing hard, which flattens the nap, and store it in a breathable bag with moth protection.
