Types, grades and fabrics

Types and grades

Read John Smedley as a maker identity before considering the fiber, yarn and knit structure of an individual item. Separate pure-fiber products from blends, and do not confuse machine gauge with fiber diameter. A collection name describes a commercial range; the composition label describes what the garment contains.

Fabrics and products

Fine knitted pullovers, cardigans and collared styles provide examples for comparing cotton and wool in similar garment forms. Intermeshed loops can give stretch without an added elastic fiber. Check whether a product is pure cotton, wool or a blend, and compare thickness and stitch density before assigning it a season.

Buying and care

How to judge quality

Inspect the neckline, cuffs and seams for consistency, then compare the body’s stitch density and weight with its intended use. Ask whether a soft surface is the result of a particular finish. Keep the product reference and fiber percentages so that future care and repair advice can be matched to the actual item.

Care in detail

Follow the complete item’s care label, including instructions for its lining, trims and finish. Support delicate knitwear when wet and avoid storing it under tension; tailored garments need care suited to their internal construction. Keep pieces clean and dry before storage, and seek repairs while damage is still limited.

Care at a glance

Follow the finished product’s care label. A trademark does not specify the care needs of every blend or construction.

Environmental and social footprint

Ask separately about raw-fiber sourcing, wet processing, energy use and the expected life of the finished item. A heritage date or prestigious address cannot summarize those impacts. Product-specific sourcing evidence is more useful than a company-wide adjective, and durability depends on construction and care as well as the fiber.

Science

Protein-based natural material

Most hair is keratin; silk is a different protein-fiber system.

Structure and chemistry

In knitwear, fiber chemistry and loop geometry contribute different properties. Wool and cashmere are protein fibers, while cotton is cellulose. Loop length, yarn size and finishing influence stretch, bulk and surface appearance. The maker’s name does not determine those variables, so examine the specified garment.

From source to yarn

In fine knitwear, yarn size, stitch length and machine gauge work together. Gauge describes the knitting equipment or construction convention; it is not the same measurement as wool micron or yarn count. Compare the neckline, cuffs, seams and body as a complete knitted garment rather than judging quality by a gauge number alone.

Performance in use

For a knitted item, compare shape recovery, surface change and comfort under its intended use. A soft first touch does not establish resistance to pilling or stretching. Knit density, yarn construction and finishing matter, and numerical performance claims require a named test on the relevant product.

How it is identified

A logo or cloth label identifies a commercial claim, not a laboratory fiber result. For identification, compare the product reference, composition label and seller documentation. Microscopy or specialist analysis may investigate fiber content, but cannot establish a particular garment maker from the fiber chemistry alone.

Labeling and law

A brand name and a fiber-content declaration answer different questions. Check the generic material names and percentages on the particular product, including relevant linings and trims. Claims about origin, a processing treatment or a sourcing program need their own documentation; the brand history supplies none of those measurements.

Frequently asked

Is Sea Island Cotton the same thing as merino?

No. Cotton is a plant cellulose fiber, while merino is sheep wool. A maker offering both does not make them interchangeable material names.

What should I ask before comparing products?

Is this cotton, merino or a blend, and what do its knit gauge and care label describe? Keep the answer tied to the item you are actually examining rather than another product from the same maker.

Does this profile verify a supply chain?

No. It records sourced brand context and reading guidance. Supplier relationships and product-level provenance require separate records for the specific item.
