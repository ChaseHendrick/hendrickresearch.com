Overview

Seed floss from milkweed plants. It functions more readily as filling than as a conventional long spinning fiber.

Feel and behavior

Light, fine and fluffy.

Where you find it

Fillings, experimental blends and insulation.

Source and geography

Milkweed floss is the seed hair (coma) of milkweeds, chiefly common milkweed, Asclepias syriaca (Apocynaceae), native to eastern North America. It is cultivated in Quebec, Canada, where researchers note that it needs no irrigation, pesticides or fertilizers. The name milkweed is also applied to seed floss of the separate genus Calotropis, including Calotropis procera and Calotropis gigantea (also Apocynaceae), called giant milkweed, which grow in arid regions of Asia and Africa and are invasive in some countries.

Advantages

  • Hollow, wax-coated fibers give very low density and good thermal insulation.
  • Common milkweed is a perennial native to eastern North America and the main larval host plant of the monarch butterfly.
  • Floss absorbs oil strongly while repelling water, which suits spill cleanup.
  • Researchers report that the Quebec crop is grown without irrigation, pesticides or fertilizers.

Drawbacks

  • Fibers are short, slippery and fragile, so floss cannot be carded or spun well alone.
  • Hollow fibers can collapse under pressure, reducing loft.
  • Supply is small and concentrated in Quebec, so products are niche and relatively expensive.
  • It is flammable like other light cellulosic floss.

Worth knowing

  • A 1944 slogan urging American schoolchildren to gather milkweed pods for life vests was 'Two bags save one life'.
  • In a field experiment, up to 90 percent of monarch eggs on potted common milkweed in perennial grasslands were lost within 72 hours, much of it at night.

Types, grades and fabrics

Types and grades

Milkweed floss is sold mainly as loose fill or as an ingredient in blended insulation. Commercial North American floss comes from cultivated common milkweed, Asclepias syriaca, grown in Quebec, and it is usually blended with other fibers because pure floss is fragile. Forms include loose floss, airlaid nonwoven batting with binder fibers, insulation for outdoor jackets and bedding, and experimental oil sorbent mats. Floss from Calotropis procera and Calotropis gigantea in Asia and Africa is also called milkweed but comes from a different genus. Grades are informal, so buyers should ask for the species, the cleaning method and the percentage of milkweed in any blend, since milkweed insulation may contain only a minority of floss.

Fabrics and products

Milkweed is used mostly where textiles need loft: insulation for outerwear, duvets, pillows and sleeping bags, usually blended with polyester or other binder fibers for durability, and building insulation and acoustic panels in nonwoven form. Spinning trials blend small percentages of floss with cotton or other fibers, because floss alone lacks cohesion. During the Second World War, floss filled life vests in place of kapok. Researchers also make oil sorbent pads from the hollow, waxy fibers.

Buying and care

How to judge quality

For fill products, check the fiber content line for the milkweed share, binder fibers and any polyester or down. Good floss is silky, white to cream and free of seeds and pod fragments. Because the hollow fibers can collapse under pressure, ask how the product performs after compression and washing; loft retention data, where available, are more informative than warmth adjectives. Pure-milkweed claims are rare and should be supported by composition data. Claims about monarch butterfly habitat describe how the plants were grown, not how the garment performs, and should be backed by farm-level information.

Care in detail

Follow the label: many milkweed-insulated jackets and duvets are designed for gentle machine washing, but the floss itself is fragile. Wash cold or lukewarm on a gentle cycle with a mild detergent suited to technical outerwear, rinse thoroughly, and skip fabric softener, which can coat fibers and reduce loft. Dry on low heat with dryer balls to break up clumps, or dry flat in shade, turning and shaking the item often until completely dry, because damp fill can mildew. Do not iron or press the insulated areas, since compression flattens hollow fibers. For storage, keep insulated items loose in a breathable bag rather than compressed in a stuff sack for long periods.

Care at a glance

Follow the complete product’s care label. Blends, dyes, finishes and construction can change suitable washing, drying and storage.

Environmental and social footprint

Common milkweed is the primary larval host plant of the monarch butterfly, whose eastern North American population fell by about 80 percent over the two decades before 2019, a decline linked in part to the loss of milkweed from farmland after herbicide-tolerant crops were adopted. Growing milkweed as a fiber crop could therefore add breeding habitat, but no study was found that measured monarch outcomes on commercial floss farms. Field studies add caveats: egg predation in grasslands reached up to 90 percent within 72 hours, and pesticide residues on milkweed near cropland produced mostly sublethal effects on monarchs in a 2020 experiment. Researchers report low input needs for the Quebec crop, but farm-scale life cycle data are lacking.

Tradeoffs

Origin, processing inputs, labor, product construction and useful lifetime all need specific evidence. The name alone does not establish responsible production.

A useful question to ask: What exact material, product form and documented producer stand behind this name?

Structure and chemistry

Each floss fiber is a single seed-hair cell, a thin-walled hollow tube whose empty channel has been measured at more than 90 percent of fiber volume, with intra-fiber porosity of about 0.83. Composition reported for Canadian milkweed is roughly 40 to 45 percent cellulose, 35 to 40 percent hemicellulose, 15 percent lignin, 3 percent free sugars and 3 percent wax. The wax makes the surface strongly hydrophobic, with water contact angles around 140 degrees, while the thin walls make fibers fragile and prone to collapse under pressure.

From source to yarn

Mature pods are collected and the floss is separated from the seeds. Because the fibers are short, smooth, brittle and low in cohesion, they card poorly: studies report that air-laying gives an overall yield of about 90 percent, far better than carding. Floss is therefore blended with binder fibers such as low-melt polyester or polylactic acid, air-laid into webs and thermally bonded, or developed into insulating fillings for winter jackets and building insulation.

Performance in use

Milkweed is valued for insulation, buoyancy and oil sorption rather than strength. Air-laid nonwovens containing 30 to 70 percent milkweed showed thermal conductivity of about 28 to 35 mW/m K at densities of only 11 to 19 kg/m3, lower than a commercial hemp insulation tested alongside, and good sound absorption. Superhydrophobic floss has absorbed up to about 100 g of oil per g. Weaknesses include fragility in carding, collapse of hollow fibers under high molding pressure, and flammability like other cellulosics.

How it is identified

Burn tests are indicative only: milkweed floss burns quickly like other light cellulosic floss. Microscopy shows smooth, straight, thin-walled hollow tubes without cotton's convolutions, very similar to kapok, so the two are hard to separate visually; seed source documentation and careful measurement of diameter and wall thickness help. Fills sold as milkweed may be blends with synthetic fibers, and FTIR or solubility tests can detect polyester.

Milkweed floss has been explored as a buoyant filling and an alternative plant fiber. Its short, smooth structure shapes both its opportunities and spinning limitations.

The deeper record

Milkweed floss has long been compared with kapok as a buoyant, insulating fill. Accounts of wartime pod-collection campaigns in North America to replace scarce kapok are widely repeated but were not verified against primary sources in this pass. Recent Canadian research has turned Quebec-grown milkweed into jacket fillings, building insulation and bio-composites for automobile dashboards, while Indian and Iranian studies have tested Asclepias and Calotropis floss as oil sorbents and filters.

Labeling and law

Milkweed is not among the Annex I fibre names in Regulation (EU) No 1007/2011, so EU labels would generally show it as other fibres with its percentage. In the US, covered textile products disclose fiber content under the Textile Fiber Products Identification Act (15 U.S.C. 70 et seq.) and 16 CFR Part 303, using the common name for natural fibers; blends with polyester or PLA binder fibers must name those generics. Fill and bedding products may be subject to additional rules, and pollinator or habitat claims should be substantiated.

Frequently asked

Is milkweed insulation as warm as down?

Tests of milkweed nonwovens found low thermal conductivity, and makers present it as an alternative to down and synthetics, but warmth depends on fill weight, blend and construction. Independent side-by-side tests of finished jackets are scarce, so compare fill weight and loft data rather than fiber names.

Does milkweed farming help monarch butterflies?

Possibly. Common milkweed is the monarch's main larval host plant, and loss of milkweed from farmland is one suspected cause of the eastern population's decline. However, no study was found measuring monarch benefits on commercial floss farms, and predation and pesticide exposure also affect monarch survival.

Why did Americans collect milkweed pods in World War II?

Imported kapok used to fill life vests became unavailable after Japan seized the East Indies. In 1944 schoolchildren and youth groups collected milkweed pods to supply floss as a substitute, under slogans such as 'Two bags save one life', and collectors were paid for each bag.
