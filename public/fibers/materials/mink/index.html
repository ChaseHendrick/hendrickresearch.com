Overview

Mink is usually traded as a fur pelt, not as a conventional spun apparel fiber.

Feel and behavior

A dense underfur and visible guard-hair surface.

Where you find it

Fur garments, panels and trim.

Source and geography

Farmed mink is the American mink, Neogale vison (formerly Neovison vison or Mustela vison), a semi-aquatic mustelid native to North America that is bred in cages for its pelt. In 2020 Denmark had about 1,100 mink farms, and more than 15 million mink were culled there late that year. Selective breeding has produced color types such as Nordic Brown and American Black, and Danish researchers have tested genomic selection for size and fur traits. Mink enters textiles as dressed pelts and trim, not as spun fiber.

Advantages

  • Dense underfur and guard hair give high warmth for weight and good wind resistance.
  • Well-dressed and stored mink garments can last many years and be remodeled.
  • The pelt surface sheds light rain and snow.
  • Mink is lighter and more supple than many longer-haired furs.

Drawbacks

  • Farming animals in cages raises welfare concerns that EFSA found could not be fully mitigated in current systems.
  • Sale and farming are restricted or banned in a growing number of jurisdictions.
  • It requires specialist cleaning and cool, ventilated storage.
  • Species and origin are hard for buyers to verify without documentation.

Worth knowing

  • The US Truth in Fur Labeling Act of 2010 ended an exemption that had let fur items with less than $150 of fur go unlabeled.
  • Studies of farmed mink found that animals raised in barren cages show more stereotypic pacing and that enrichment reduces it.
  • California became the first US state to ban the sale and manufacture of new fur products, effective 1 January 2023.

Types, grades and fabrics

Types and grades

Mink is traded as pelts graded by sex, size, color, clarity and fur quality. Male pelts are larger with longer, coarser guard hair; female pelts are smaller, lighter and often finer, and garment makers choose between them for weight and drape. Farmed color types range from dark browns and blacks to paler mutation colors such as pastel and white, the product of generations of selective breeding. Pelts may be left natural, dyed, bleached, sheared to a velvety pile or plucked of guard hair. Construction also defines grade: full-skin garments, let-out garments in which pelts are cut into narrow diagonal strips and resewn to lengthen them, and cheaper pieced goods assembled from sections, paws or tails. Knitted mink uses narrow fur strips worked into a knit.

Fabrics and products

Mink is used as dressed skins rather than spun fiber. Full-length coats, jackets, stoles and capes are the traditional products, typically lined with silk or synthetic satin. Collars, cuffs and hood trims attach mink to wool or down outerwear, and pompoms and key chains use off-cuts. Sheared mink, trimmed to an even short pile, is used reversibly or with lighter linings. Knitted or woven mink cuts pelts into long thin strips that are knitted or woven with a yarn base, making lighter scarves and throws. Mink is also used in accessories such as hats, gloves and bag trims. Some cosmetic brushes and eyelash products have used mink hair, a separate market with its own labeling questions.

Buying and care

How to judge quality

Quality shows in dense, even underfur that springs back when blown on, lustrous guard hairs of uniform length, and a soft, supple leather side that does not crackle. Tug gently: more than a few loose hairs suggests poor dressing or age. Matched color and seams that lie flat indicate careful construction; let-out work should show fine, even seams. Stiffness, odor, yellowing or tears in the leather indicate aging. In the US, the Fur Products Labeling Act requires labels to give the animal name, country of origin for imported fur, and whether the fur is dyed or bleached, pieced from paws, tails or bellies, or used. A missing or vague label is a red flag. Price depends heavily on construction and whether the pelts are whole.

Care in detail

Fur needs specialist care, and the label and a qualified furrier govern. Store mink on a broad, shaped hanger in a breathable cloth bag, never plastic, in a cool, dark, ventilated place; many owners use professional cold storage in warm months to protect against heat, humidity and insects. Keep it away from perfume, hairspray and shoulder-bag straps, which abrade and stain. If it gets wet, shake it out and hang it to dry at room temperature away from radiators; do not brush or use a hair dryer. Never iron, machine wash or home dry clean it. Have it professionally cleaned about once a year and small tears repaired promptly before they spread. Heat, light and dryness cause the leather to become brittle.

Care at a glance

Use a specialist familiar with the material and construction; avoid improvised washing.

Environmental and social footprint

Mink impact debates center on animal welfare, disease and processing. The 2025 EFSA opinion found that key welfare consequences of cage systems could not be prevented within them, and experimental work shows farmed mink in non-enriched cages develop more stereotypic behavior, such as pacing, than those given enrichment. SARS-CoV-2 outbreaks on mink farms in 2020 showed how dense animal husbandry can create zoonotic risk. Environmentally, mink are carnivores fed on animal by-products, and pelts must be dressed and often dyed with chemical treatments; this review found no independent, peer-reviewed life cycle comparison of mink with faux fur, so claims on either side should be treated as contested. Legal context is shifting: California banned the sale of new fur products from 2023.

Tradeoffs

Animal welfare and traceability are central. Original map connections are retained as unverified legacy concepts.

A useful question to ask: Can the seller document the species, provenance and handling at every stage?

Science

Species or trade-specific material

Public specifications and independently verified sourcing can be limited.

Structure and chemistry

A mink pelt is skin carrying two hair populations: long, stiff guard hairs and a dense underfur, called wool in mink grading, that provides insulation. Graders score guard hair length, guard hair thickness, underfur density, silky appearance and overall quality; these traits show low to moderate heritability, with quantitative trait loci mapped on several chromosomes. Selection for underfur density improved guard hair quality and almost eliminated the defect called metallic sheen. The hairs are keratin, with medullated guard hairs, and the skin is a collagen network that must be stabilized by dressing.

From source to yarn

The winter coat grows during the growing-furring period, from about mid-September until pelting in December. Diet matters: cutting dietary protein reduced the number and diameter of under hairs and guard hairs and shortened pelts, and anemia in young kits leads to the cotton fur defect. Animals are graded alive, killed and pelted, and the dried skins are graded again and sold. Pelts are then dressed, a tanning process that stabilizes the skin while retaining the hair, before being matched and sewn into garments or trim.

Performance in use

Fur insulates by trapping still air in the dense underfur, while guard hairs shed water and protect the underfur from abrasion. Guard hairs break and underfur mats with wear, and the dressed skin is sensitive to heat, water and solvents, which is why specialist cleaning is advised. In a laboratory study, SARS-CoV-2 remained infectious for up to 10 days on mink fur, longer than on cotton or faux fur; UV light failed to inactivate it on pelts, whereas heating at 60 C for one hour inactivated it on all tested surfaces.

How it is identified

Burn tests on a few hairs (indicative only, and to be done with care) show keratin behavior, whereas acrylic or modacrylic faux fur melts; checking for a skin rather than a knitted or woven backing at the hair base is a simpler non-destructive clue. Species identification uses guard hair microscopy (scale pattern, medulla and cross-section) against reference hairs, or DNA analysis. Dyeing and shearing can disguise species, so pelts should be matched to documentation.

Mink’s commercial history primarily concerns pelts and fur garments. The original atlas grouped it with fibers; this edition retains it in a clearly separated specialty archive. Current trade links and welfare claims have not been validated here.

The deeper record

Fur farming as a whole began in the mid-nineteenth century. The modern turning point was SARS-CoV-2: infection in farmed mink was detected in the Netherlands in April 2020 and in Denmark in June, where 290 farms became infected, mink-associated variants including the Cluster 5 lineage spread to an estimated 4,000 people, and the government culled the entire population of more than 15 million mink. In 2025 a European Food Safety Authority opinion concluded that key welfare consequences of current cage systems, including restricted movement and inability to explore or forage, could not be prevented or substantially mitigated within those systems.

Labeling and law

In the US, fur apparel falls under the Fur Products Labeling Act, whose FTC rules at 16 CFR Part 301 govern how the animal name and other information appear on labels, invoices and advertising. In the EU, Regulation (EU) No 1007/2011 requires textile products that contain non-textile parts of animal origin, such as fur trim, to state this on the label. After SARS-CoV-2 reached farmed mink, entire national mink populations were culled and production banned in some countries; Denmark enacted an interim ban on mink production until the end of 2021 after culling its entire herd in 2020.

This describes what rules and standards cover. It is not legal advice; jurisdiction, product form and current rule text control.

Frequently asked

How can I tell real mink from faux fur?

Part the hair at the base. Real fur grows from leather skin, while faux fur is attached to a knitted or woven fabric backing. Real hair tapers to a fine point and a singed hair smells of burnt hair, whereas acrylic faux fur melts. A label is required in the US for fur products.

Is it legal to sell mink in California?

Since 1 January 2023, California law (AB 44) has prohibited selling, offering, displaying for sale or manufacturing new fur products, with exceptions such as used or vintage fur, religious and tribal use, and certain species. Other jurisdictions have their own rules, so check the local law.

How long does a mink coat last?

With professional cleaning and proper cool storage, a mink coat can last for decades, and furriers can remodel or reline it. Heat, dryness and improper storage make the leather brittle and cause hair loss. Visible tears, stiffness or heavy shedding signal that the leather is deteriorating.

What does 'let-out' mink mean?

Letting out is a furrier technique in which each pelt is cut into narrow diagonal strips and sewn back together offset, making the skin longer and narrower. It creates long, flowing vertical lines in a coat and uses skilled labor, which is why let-out garments usually cost more than those made from whole or pieced skins.
