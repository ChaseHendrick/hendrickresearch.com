Overview

Production: about 0.3 million tonnes (roughly 1% of cotton production) (global recycled cotton, pre- and post-consumer, 2024). [1]

Cotton recovered from production scraps or used textiles and reopened for another textile cycle.

Feel and behavior

Input-dependent, often blended for strength.

Where you find it

Denim, knitwear, wiping cloth, insulation and blends.

Source and geography

Recycled cotton is cellulose fiber recovered from cotton textile waste. Pre-consumer (post-industrial) inputs include spinning and weaving waste, cutting-room scraps and finishing waste such as brush fiber from sueding; post-consumer inputs are used garments and home textiles. Research literature commonly cites estimates that about 75 percent of textile waste is landfilled and that less than 1 percent is recycled back into new garments, so fiber-to-fiber cotton recycling remains small relative to virgin cotton. Much recent industrial research on recycled cotton yarns comes from spinning mills and universities in Bangladesh.

Advantages

  • It reuses cotton already grown, avoiding new irrigation, fertilizer and pesticide inputs for that share.
  • Pre-colored inputs can skip dyeing and give melange effects.
  • It performs like cotton in absorbency, breathability and ease of care.
  • Pre-consumer scrap is a clean, well-characterized feedstock that is otherwise wasted.

Drawbacks

  • Shredding shortens fibers, so yarns are weaker, hairier and usually coarser.
  • It usually needs blending with virgin cotton or polyester, which dilutes the recycled share and can complicate later recycling.
  • Post-consumer inputs vary in color, finishes and hidden elastane or polyester.
  • Claims are hard to verify without certificates, because recycled and virgin cotton look the same in tests.

Worth knowing

  • Textile Exchange estimates that recycled textiles, pre- and post-consumer combined, make up less than 1% of the global fiber market.
  • Mixed-color cotton scraps can be respun into heathered yarns with no new dyeing at all.
  • The Swedish cotton-to-pulp recycler Renewcell went bankrupt and was bought by the investment firm Altor, showing how fragile chemical recycling economics still are.

Types, grades and fabrics

Types and grades

Recycled cotton is separated first by source. Pre-consumer (post-industrial) fiber comes from spinning waste, cutting-room scraps and off-cuts; it is clean, known in composition and often sorted by color. Post-consumer fiber comes from used garments and home textiles and is more variable, with dyes, finishes and blended fibers. Mechanically recycled cotton is shredded back to short fiber and respun; color-sorted inputs can give melange yarns with no redyeing. Chemically recycled cotton is not sold as cotton: cotton-rich waste is dissolved into pulp for regenerated fibers such as lyocell or viscose. The Swedish chemical recycler Renewcell, which made such pulp, entered bankruptcy and was acquired by the investment firm Altor in 2024, a reminder that the sector is still commercially fragile.

Fabrics and products

Because mechanically recycled fibers are short, they are usually blended and rotor spun into coarser yarns for denim, sweatshirts and fleece, T-shirts, socks, towels, blankets and canvas. Recycled cotton is often combined with virgin cotton for fineness or with polyester, frequently recycled polyester, for strength. Studies have demonstrated ring-spun yarns with a quarter or so recycled content for finer knits. Heather and speckled melange effects come naturally from mixed pre-colored inputs. Lower grades go into mop yarns, wiping cloths, industrial felts, insulation batts and upholstery padding, which are common destinations for post-consumer cotton that cannot be respun.

Buying and care

How to judge quality

Expect recycled cotton fabric to feel slightly drier and hairier than premium virgin cotton, with more surface fuzz and occasional flecks from mixed inputs. Check the full fiber line: a garment described as recycled may contain only a minority share of recycled cotton, blended with virgin cotton or polyester. Pull the fabric gently and look at yarn evenness; very uneven, thin areas or high lint suggest short-fiber-heavy yarn. For denim, a firm, dense twill hides the shorter fibers well. Credible claims name a percentage and reference a chain-of-custody certificate such as the Recycled Claim Standard or Global Recycled Standard with transaction certificates. Because recycled and virgin cotton cannot be told apart by testing, documentation is the only proof.

Care in detail

Care for recycled cotton as you would cotton, with a little extra gentleness. Wash at 30 to 40 C for everyday items, turning garments inside out to limit surface abrasion and lint; hotter washes up to the label limit suit towels and bedding. Use a normal or color detergent, and avoid chlorine bleach on melange colors. Short fibers make recycled fabrics lint and pill more at first, so wash with similar fabrics and clean the lint filter often. Tumble dry low to medium or line dry to limit shrinkage. Iron pure cotton hot (three dots, about 200 C) while slightly damp, but use two dots if polyester is blended in. Store dry and clean to prevent mildew.

Care at a glance

Follow the complete product’s care label. Blends, dyes, finishes and construction can change suitable washing, drying and storage.

Environmental and social footprint

Recycled cotton's benefit comes mainly from displacing virgin cotton farming, so it scales with the recycled share. A 2023 cradle-to-gate life cycle assessment comparing fabrics with 30% mechanically recycled and 70% virgin cotton against 100% virgin cotton found reductions that were real but modest, in the single digits for greenhouse gases in several scenarios; larger savings require higher recycled shares and clean energy. Textile-to-textile recycling is still marginal: Textile Exchange reports that pre- and post-consumer recycled textiles made up less than 1% of the global fiber market. Collection, sorting and transport add impacts, and blending with virgin polyester to restore strength shifts rather than removes the footprint. Evidence is highly dependent on system boundaries and allocation choices.

Tradeoffs

Origin, processing inputs, labor, product construction and useful lifetime all need specific evidence. The name alone does not establish responsible production.

A useful question to ask: What exact material, product form and documented producer stand behind this name?

Science

Recovered material, retained chemistry

Recycled describes the feedstock history, not a new polymer family.

Structure and chemistry

Chemically, recycled cotton is still cellulose, a linear polymer of beta-1,4-linked glucose, but mechanical recycling changes its physical profile. Shredding tears fabric apart, breaking fibers, lowering mean length and raising short-fiber content, neps and leftover yarn fragments; a shredder described in one study reduced fabric to individual fibers no longer than about 35 mm. Dyed inputs keep their color, and residual finishes, elastane or polyester may be present from mixed fabrics. Dust and trash particles can carry into yarn and later appear on the fabric surface.

From source to yarn

Inputs are sorted by fiber content and color, trims such as buttons, zippers and labels are removed, and fabric is cut and shredded through rotating toothed cylinders into loose fiber. Arafat and Uddin (2022) note that because this fiber is short, it has traditionally been blended and rotor (open-end) spun into coarse yarns of about 10 to 20 Ne for denim, towels and home furnishings. In an industrial trial they showed that up to about 25 percent recycled cotton could be blended with virgin cotton in soft-twist 30 Ne ring-spun yarn suitable for knit tops. Chemical routes instead dissolve cotton-rich waste to make pulp for regenerated cellulosic fibers.

Performance in use

Performance depends on the input and blend. Shorter fibers mean more hairiness, lower yarn strength and more unevenness: a 2026 study of rotor yarns with brush-fiber waste found higher mass variation, more imperfections and hairiness and slightly lower tensile properties than 100 percent virgin cotton. Rotor yarns need high twist, which makes them stiffer and less absorbent and gives a harsher hand from surface wrapper fibers. Otherwise recycled cotton behaves like cotton: hydrophilic, stronger wet than dry, flammable, and dyeable with reactive, direct and vat dyes. Pre-colored inputs can avoid dyeing and give melange effects.

How it is identified

Recycled cotton cannot be distinguished from virgin cotton by burn testing (indicative only; both burn quickly with a paper-like smell and soft grey ash) or by FTIR, since both are cellulose. Length testing reveals a larger short-fiber share and microscopy may show more broken ends and fragments, but neither proves recycled origin. Recycled-content claims therefore rest on chain-of-custody documentation rather than fiber testing, while contamination by polyester or elastane from mixed inputs can be detected by solubility tests or FTIR.

Recovering cotton textiles predates modern circular-fashion language. Current systems differ in input sorting, color separation and retained fiber length.

The deeper record

Reclaiming fiber from textile waste has a long history, including the nineteenth-century trade in shoddy made from wool rags and the use of cotton and linen rags for papermaking before wood pulp. Cotton spinning waste has long gone into coarse yarns, wiping cloths and batting. Current work aims to raise recycled shares in finer yarns: studies since 2022 have tested ring spinning with pre- and post-consumer recycled cotton, direct use of pre-colored brush-fiber waste in rotor yarns, and the contamination that cotton waste yarns leave on woven fabric surfaces.

Labeling and law

The generic fiber name remains cotton under the US Textile Fiber Products Identification Act and Regulation (EU) No 1007/2011. In the US, a recycled claim is also an environmental marketing claim, and the FTC's Green Guides (16 CFR Part 260) require recycled-content claims to be substantiated. Textile Exchange's Recycled Claim Standard and Global Recycled Standard are voluntary chain-of-custody certifications that verify recycled input, with the Global Recycled Standard adding social, environmental and chemical requirements. Fibers regenerated from dissolved cotton waste are labeled as regenerated cellulosic fibers, not as cotton.

Frequently asked

Is recycled cotton as good as new cotton?

It behaves like cotton in comfort and absorbency, but mechanical recycling shortens fibers, so fabrics can be weaker, hairier and coarser. Makers usually blend it with virgin cotton or polyester to compensate. For heavy fabrics such as denim and sweatshirts the difference is small; for fine shirting it is harder to use.

Why is recycled cotton often blended with polyester?

Shredded cotton fibers are short and give weaker yarn, so polyester, often recycled polyester, adds strength and spinning stability. The trade-off is that the resulting blend is harder to recycle again, because separating cotton from polyester requires chemical processes that are not yet widespread.

How can I tell if a recycled cotton claim is real?

Look for a stated percentage and a chain-of-custody certification such as the Recycled Claim Standard or Global Recycled Standard. Laboratory tests cannot distinguish recycled from virgin cotton, so documentation through the supply chain is the only verification. Vague phrases without percentages or certificates deserve skepticism.

Does recycled cotton save water?

It avoids the irrigation used to grow the cotton it replaces, so it can save substantial water per kilogram of fiber. At the garment level, savings depend on how much recycled fiber is used; with about 30% recycled content, one 2023 study found only modest overall reductions.
