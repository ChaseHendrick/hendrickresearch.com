Overview

Fine undercoat recovered from bison and separated from coarse hair.

Feel and behavior

Soft, warm down after preparation.

Where you find it

Small-scale yarns, accessories and blends.

Source and geography

Bison down is the winter undercoat of the American bison (Bison bison), in commerce mainly plains bison (B. b. bison) from privately managed ranch herds in the United States and Canada. Fiber is gathered as shed fiber in spring, when some ranches allow collectors to pick it, or recovered by other producer-specific methods that buyers should ask about. Hedrick (2009) estimated about 500,000 plains bison in North America, of which only about 4 percent (roughly 20,000) were in conservation herds, so nearly all commercial fiber comes from private herds. Supply is small and tied to individual producers.

Advantages

  • Fine dehaired down is soft enough for next-to-skin accessories.
  • Fibers hold still air, giving good warmth for their weight.
  • Fiber is a by-product of ranch herds kept for meat and conservation, not a separate harvest.
  • Natural brown color allows undyed yarns.

Drawbacks

  • Short staple makes 100 percent yarns prone to shedding and pilling.
  • Supply is small and depends on individual ranches, so price is high and color lots vary.
  • Natural brown limits pale dyed shades.
  • Species claims are hard to verify without laboratory testing.

Worth knowing

  • Bison down yarns are commonly sold in lace and fingering weights because the short down suits fine, well-twisted yarns.
  • At least one US producer sells a 50 percent bison down and 50 percent mulberry silk laceweight, using silk to add strength and drape to the short down.
  • Bison is not a specialty fiber under the Wool Products Labeling Act, so US labels name it as bison fiber or hair rather than wool.

Types, grades and fabrics

Types and grades

Commercial bison fiber comes almost entirely from plains bison (Bison bison bison) on private ranches. Trade forms are graded by how much guard hair has been removed: raw shed fiber, dehaired down, carded roving or batts for hand spinners, and finished yarn. Yarns are sold as 100 percent bison down or as blends. Blends with silk, merino or cashmere are common because the short down spins more easily with a longer partner; one small US producer, for example, sells a laceweight of 50 percent bison down and 50 percent mulberry silk. Most bison yarn is lace, fingering or sport weight, sold in small skeins.

Fabrics and products

Bison down is used mainly in hand-knitting yarns and in small runs of knitted accessories such as scarves, hats, gloves and socks, plus some woven scarves and throws. Lace and fingering weights suit shawls and lightweight accessories where the down's warmth per gram matters most. Blends with silk add strength, luster and drape; merino blends add elasticity and memory; cotton or nylon may be added to sock yarns for abrasion resistance. Undyed natural brown is the norm, and dyed bison is usually overdyed in dark shades because the base is brown.

Buying and care

How to judge quality

Look for stated 100 percent bison down or a clear blend percentage, and ask whether the fiber was dehaired commercially. Good yarn feels soft against the neck with no stiff, dark guard hairs poking out; if you can see coarse hairs, the dehairing was incomplete. A slight halo is normal, but yarn that sheds heavily when rubbed between fingers may be under-twisted. Reported down diameters of roughly 15 to 22 microns put it near cashmere, so prices well below cashmere are a red flag for a low bison percentage. Because Bison fiber is not a Wool Act specialty fiber, the label should name bison fiber or bison hair with its percentage.

Care in detail

Treat bison as a delicate, felting-prone keratin fiber. Hand wash in cool water (about 30 C or below) with a wool or hair detergent, soak briefly, and avoid rubbing or sudden temperature changes. Rinse at the same temperature, press out water in a towel and dry flat in shape, away from heaters and sun. Do not tumble dry. Steam lightly rather than ironing; if ironing is needed use a low wool setting and a pressing cloth. Remove pills gently with a fine comb. Store clean in breathable bags with a moth deterrent, and check for insect larvae periodically. Avoid chlorine bleach and alkaline detergents. The product label governs.

Care at a glance

Follow the complete product’s care label. Blends, dyes, finishes and construction can change suitable washing, drying and storage.

Environmental and social footprint

Bison fiber is a minor co-product of ranching for meat and of conservation herds; ranch managers and conservation groups argue that bison grazing can support prairie ecosystems, but no life cycle assessment of bison yarn was found in this review, so any carbon or land-use comparison with other fibers is unquantified. Collection of shed fiber in spring adds little direct burden on the animals. The main processing inputs are water and energy for scouring and mechanical dehairing, often done by small mills. Most plains bison carry some cattle ancestry and descend from a small founder population, so conservation value varies by herd; claims that a purchase supports bison conservation should name the herd or program.

Tradeoffs

Origin, processing inputs, labor, product construction and useful lifetime all need specific evidence. The name alone does not establish responsible production.

A useful question to ask: What exact material, product form and documented producer stand behind this name?

Protein-based natural material

Most hair is keratin; silk is a different protein-fiber system.

Structure and chemistry

The bison coat has two fractions: coarse outer guard hair and a dense, fine undercoat. Reported down diameters vary by source and animal, with fiber businesses citing means from roughly 15 to 22 microns and one account an average near 18.5 microns, which overlaps the range of commercial cashmere. Staple length is short, commonly cited at about 1.5 inches (about 4 cm). Like other mammalian hair it is alpha-keratin, with a cuticle of overlapping scales and a cortex of keratin intermediate filaments embedded in a sulfur-rich protein matrix. In fine undercoat fibers the medulla is typically reduced or absent, while guard hairs are thicker and usually medullated.

From source to yarn

Raw bison fiber arrives mixed with guard hair, dander, dust and plant matter. Processing follows the specialty-fiber route: hand sorting, gentle scouring with mild detergents, and mechanical dehairing, in which repeated carding-type actions exploit differences in length, diameter and stiffness so that guard hairs drop out. The short, slippery down is then carded and usually spun woollen-style, often blended with longer fibers such as merino, silk or cotton to aid drafting and stretch a scarce input. A US patent (No. 6,330,786) on buffalo hair yarn and fabric describes blending bison fiber with compatible fibers to extend quantity and add properties.

Performance in use

Dehaired bison down gives substantial warmth for its weight because fine, crimped fibers hold still air, and it handles moisture like other keratin fibers, absorbing water vapor while still feeling dry. Fibers below about 20 microns are generally too fine to trigger the prickle response. Its short staple is the main weakness: yarns can shed and pill unless well twisted or blended, and abrasion resistance depends on the blend partner. It chars rather than melts. It accepts acid and other protein dye classes, although the natural brown is often left undyed. It shares protein-fiber vulnerabilities to moth larvae, alkaline detergents, chlorine bleach and felting under heat and agitation.

How it is identified

Burn behavior (indicative only; test small samples with care) is that of hair: slow burning, a burnt-hair odor and brittle ash. Under the microscope bison down appears as fine, pigmented keratin fibers, and any residual guard hairs are far thicker and medullated. Microscopy alone may not separate bison down from yak or other bovid down reliably, so origin claims in high-value goods should rest on DNA-based species testing, which is used for luxury animal fibers, together with documentation from the ranch or processor.

Bison materials are part of Indigenous North American histories. Modern down yarn is a specialized product and should not be treated as a uniform account of those varied traditions.

The deeper record

North American bison numbered in the many millions in the mid-nineteenth century and were reduced to near extinction by the middle 1880s. Hedrick (2009) notes that plains bison survived mainly through five private ranch herds and a small herd in what is now Yellowstone National Park, so today's plains bison descend from fewer than 100 founders, and many herds carry cattle ancestry from crossbreeding promoted in the late 1800s and early 1900s. Conservation genetics continues: a 2022 mtDNA survey of 14 US and Canadian herds and a 2026 genome-wide study of herds reintroduced at American Prairie from 2005 onward. The down-yarn niche is recent and depends on this ranching base.

Labeling and law

Bison is not among the specialty fibers named in the Wool Products Labeling Act, so bison content is disclosed under the Textile Fiber Products Identification Act rules. Under 16 CFR 303.6, the name of a fur-bearing animal may be used with the word fiber, hair or blend when that fiber is 5 percent or more of the product, and hair of animals other than those the rule lists may be described collectively as fur fiber when no representation is made about the animal. In the EU, Regulation (EU) No 1007/2011 places such hair in the animal or horsehair category, where the animal may be named.

This describes what rules and standards cover. It is not legal advice; jurisdiction, product form and current rule text control.

Frequently asked

Is bison wool warmer than cashmere?

Producers often claim so, but no standardized comparative test was found. Dehaired bison down has fiber diameters overlapping cashmere, so warmth per gram is likely similar; knit structure, yarn thickness and blend partner affect warmth more than the species difference.

Are bison harmed to collect the fiber?

Commercial bison fiber is collected as shed fiber in spring or recovered from hides of animals raised for meat, depending on the supplier. Bison are not routinely shorn. Ask the seller which method applies to the product.

Why is bison yarn so expensive?

Each animal gives a small amount of usable down, the down must be separated from guard hair on specialized dehairing equipment, and production runs are tiny. These costs resemble those of qiviut and cashmere.

Can bison yarn be dyed?

Yes. Bison down takes acid dyes and other protein-fiber dye classes, but the natural brown base limits results to darker or muted shades. Bleaching can lighten it but weakens the keratin, so most bison yarn is sold undyed or overdyed in deep colors.
