Overview

A proprietary yarn technology commonly associated with wool blends. It changes yarn construction rather than naming a new animal fiber.

Feel and behavior

Depends on the specified fiber grade, yarn and fabric construction.

Where you find it

See the linked underlying material and the producer’s product-specific documentation.

Source and geography

Nuyarn is a proprietary yarn-construction technology, not a fiber, and the name is a trademark. It is marketed mainly for yarns that combine merino wool from the domestic sheep, Ovis aries, with a continuous synthetic filament, typically a polyamide (nylon), so the generic fibers are usually wool and nylon. Public, independently verifiable information on the owning company, its manufacturing sites and its licensing arrangements is limited, and this entry could not confirm those details from primary sources; the producer's own site is the starting point for such questions.

Advantages

  • The nylon carrier adds strength and abrasion resistance to fine merino, which is weak on its own.
  • Twist-free construction keeps the wool lofty, which the brand links to warmth for weight and faster drying.
  • The producer reports test results showing much stronger seams than conventional ring-spun merino.
  • Garments resist spirality, the twisting of side seams that can occur with twisted yarns, according to the brand.

Drawbacks

  • The nylon filament makes garments a wool-synthetic blend that is harder to recycle and not fully biodegradable.
  • Public, independent technical data beyond brand-reported tests are limited.
  • Wool sourcing and animal welfare are not covered by the technology name.
  • Garments usually cost more than conventional merino of similar weight.

Worth knowing

  • Nuyarn drafts wool fibers along a nylon filament without twisting them, the opposite of conventional spinning, which relies on twist to hold staple fibers together.
  • The brand reports tests by Intertek and AgResearch finding seams about 120% stronger than in conventional ring-spun merino garments.

Types, grades and fabrics

Types and grades

Nuyarn is a yarn-making method rather than a set of fiber grades. As trade reporting describes it, superfine merino fibers are drafted along a continuous high-performance nylon filament that acts as a carrier, without twisting the wool, producing a two-ply style yarn with more volume and air than a twisted ring-spun yarn. Brand and retailer materials describe it as developed in New Zealand in the mid-2010s. Yarns differ mainly by the merino fineness used, the filament's denier and the wool-to-nylon ratio, which is typically wool-dominant; the exact percentages appear on each garment's label. Weights range from light base-layer jerseys to heavier midlayer and sock yarns.

Fabrics and products

Nuyarn yarns are knitted into merino base layers, T-shirts, leggings, midlayers, hunting and outdoor layers, activewear and socks. Several New Zealand outdoor and activewear brands use it for technical merino ranges, often presenting it as an alternative to merino blended with elastane. Brands pitch it for garments where fine merino alone wears quickly, such as socks, active layers and pieces worn under pack straps. Knit structures are the usual single jersey, rib and interlock of merino apparel, so fabric weight and gauge still decide warmth and durability.

Buying and care

How to judge quality

Read the label for the wool and nylon percentages and for the merino micron if given; a Nuyarn garment will not be '100% merino'. Brands using the technology typically name it on hangtags or in product descriptions; ask the brand if unsure. The company reports independent testing by Intertek and AgResearch showing seams about 120% stronger than in conventional ring-spun 100% merino; ask the garment brand for its own fabric test results on pilling, abrasion and dimensional stability. Handle the fabric: it should feel lofty and soft, recover when stretched, and show no twisting of side seams after washing. Check for mulesing-free or certified wool claims separately, since Nuyarn does not cover wool sourcing.

Care in detail

Care follows merino with a synthetic core. Machine wash on a wool or gentle cycle at 30 C or cooler, inside out, with a wool-safe detergent that contains no protease enzymes or bleach, which attack keratin. Avoid fabric softener, which coats fibers. Dry flat or line dry in the shade; tumble drying risks felting and shrinkage of the wool component unless the label allows it. Iron rarely, on a wool setting with steam and a press cloth. Air garments between wears, since merino resists odor. Store clean and dry with cedar or sealed bags, because clothes moths and carpet beetles still attack the wool fraction.

Care at a glance

Follow the finished product’s care label. A trademark does not specify the care needs of every blend or construction.

Environmental and social footprint

Nuyarn's environmental profile is essentially that of its components: merino wool, with the grazing land use, enteric methane and animal welfare questions of sheep farming, plus a nylon filament made from fossil feedstocks unless recycled nylon is specified. No life-cycle assessment of Nuyarn yarn was found for this entry. The blend has an end-of-life cost: wool alone can biodegrade and can be mechanically recycled, but an intimate wool-nylon construction complicates both. If the stronger yarn lets garments last longer, that could offset some impact, but this has not been independently quantified. For animal welfare, buyers should look for wool sourcing claims such as mulesing-free or certified standards, which research on flystrike control shows matter to the animals: alternatives to surgical mulesing have measurably smaller welfare effects on lambs.

Science

Protein-based natural material

Most hair is keratin; silk is a different protein-fiber system.

Structure and chemistry

The fiber chemistry is simply that of the components. Merino wool is alpha-keratin: helical protein chains cross-linked by cystine disulfide bonds, organized into orthocortical and paracortical cells inside an overlapping cuticle of scales, with fine merino commonly in the high teens to low twenties of microns. Nylon filament is an aliphatic polyamide whose chains are held together by amide hydrogen bonds. What the technology changes, according to its marketing, is how the staple wool is arranged around and held by the filament, not the structure of either fiber.

From source to yarn

Conventional wool yarns rely on twist to bind staple fibers, and twist compacts the yarn. Nuyarn is marketed as an alternative in which drafted wool fibers are assembled around a continuous filament core with little or no twist, relying on the filament for strength so that the wool can stay lofty. The precise machinery and settings are proprietary and could not be verified here. Knitting, dyeing and finishing then follow normal wool-blend practice, and the chosen chemistry must suit both the keratin and the polyamide.

Performance in use

Performance should be judged from fabric test reports rather than from the yarn name. In principle, lower twist can increase bulk and trapped air, which affects warmth, drying time and hand, while a nylon core adds tensile strength and abrasion resistance. Wool contributes high moisture regain, charring and self-extinguishing flame behavior and susceptibility to felting and to clothes moth and carpet beetle larvae; nylon melts rather than chars. Claims about faster drying, stretch or durability are producer statements tied to particular comparisons and constructions.

How it is identified

Burn tests (indicative only, and to be done with care) on separated components show wool burning slowly with a burnt-hair odor and crushable ash, and nylon melting to a hard bead. Microscopy reveals scaled wool fibers laid around smooth synthetic filaments. Quantitative blend analysis uses selective dissolution of one component. No laboratory method identifies the Nuyarn brand itself; confusion arises when the name is treated as a fiber, or when a wool and nylon yarn is described as pure merino.

The deeper record

Nuyarn belongs to a longer history of efforts to change how wool yarn is built rather than which fiber is used: core-spun, wrap-spun and low-twist yarns have long been developed to add strength or bulk. The technology is promoted mainly to outdoor and performance apparel brands for merino base layers, socks and midlayers. Company founding dates, inventors and ownership could not be verified from primary sources in this pass, so they are omitted rather than guessed.

Labeling and law

Nuyarn is a trademark for a yarn technology, not a generic fiber name, so a label must still state the fibers and percentages, for example wool and nylon. In the US, wool content falls under the Wool Products Labeling Act of 1939 and FTC rules at 16 CFR Part 300, and other fibers under the Textile Fiber Products Identification Act and 16 CFR Part 303. In the EU, Regulation (EU) No 1007/2011 requires the listed names, such as wool and polyamide or nylon. Any wool-sourcing certification is a separate claim to verify.

This describes what rules and standards cover. It is not legal advice; jurisdiction, product form and current rule text control.

Frequently asked

Is Nuyarn 100% merino?

No. Nuyarn yarns combine merino wool with a continuous nylon filament that carries the wool. The garment label will list both, usually with wool as the majority. The technology changes how the yarn is built, not the fibers themselves.

Why do brands use Nuyarn instead of merino with elastane?

The brands say the nylon-carrier, twist-free yarn gives durability, loft and some shape retention without elastane, while keeping the wool feel. Whether it performs better for a given use depends on the fabric, so compare test data and reviews.

How do I wash Nuyarn merino?

Like fine merino: cool gentle wash with a wool detergent, no bleach or enzyme detergents, and flat or line drying. The nylon adds strength, but the wool can still felt with heat and agitation, and moths can still damage it.
