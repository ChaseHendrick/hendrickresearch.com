Source and geography

ZQ is a wool sourcing and assurance program associated with New Zealand merino growers and the brands that buy from them. It is not a sheep breed, species or generic fiber: the fiber is wool, most commonly merino wool from the domestic sheep, Ovis aries. The program sets standards for participating farms and traces wool through the supply chain. This entry could not verify the operating company, launch date or current standard version from primary sources, so readers should consult the program's own documentation for those details.

Advantages

  • The base standard prohibits mulesing, according to the program.
  • Farms are audited by a third party against published animal welfare, environmental and social requirements.
  • The ZQ+ regenerative program adds measured farm-level outcomes beyond a pass or fail audit.
  • Traceability links wool to participating growers, mainly in New Zealand.

Drawbacks

  • The mark does not specify fiber quality, so ZQ wool can still be coarse or prickly if the grade is wrong.
  • Program standards are set by the commercial company that sells the wool, not by an independent standards body.
  • Regenerative outcomes are program-defined metrics whose independent verification is still developing.
  • Certified wool usually costs more than conventional wool.

Worth knowing

  • The company behind ZQ renamed itself Zentera, and its regenerative program ZQRX was renamed ZQ+.
  • The program describes ZQ as the first accredited wool fiber to guarantee that mulesing does not occur, a claim based on its farm audits.

Types, grades and fabrics

Types and grades

ZQ is a set of wool sourcing standards, now run by Zentera, the company formerly called The New Zealand Merino Company, which has worked with growers under the ZQ name since 2007. The base ZQ standard is a third-party audited farm standard covering animal welfare, environmental integrity and social responsibility; it prohibits mulesing. ZQ+, formerly called ZQRX (the ZQ Regenerative Index), builds on ZQ with a regenerative agriculture program that measures and improves farm outcomes over time. Zentera also supplies wool certified to Textile Exchange's Responsible Wool Standard. None of these grades the fiber itself: micron, staple length and strength are specified separately, and ZQ is best known for fine merino.

Fabrics and products

ZQ merino goes into the same products as other fine wool: base layers, T-shirts, socks, knitwear, activewear, outdoor midlayers and suiting. Outdoor and performance apparel brands have been prominent users; Helly Hansen, for example, has described bringing ZQRX (now ZQ+) wool into base layers. Because the standard concerns farms and supply, the fabrics are ordinary wool constructions: worsted jersey, interlock and rib knits, woollen-spun sweaters and worsted suiting, sometimes blended with nylon or elastane.

Buying and care

How to judge quality

The ZQ or ZQ+ mark on a hangtag indicates the wool came from audited growers, but it says nothing about fineness, so read the label for wool percentage and, if given, micron. For merino next to skin, fibers finer than about 20 microns generally feel soft, while coarser wool can prickle. Ask the brand whether the whole wool content is ZQ or only part of it, and check whether blends include nylon or elastane. Genuine program participation can be confirmed with the brand or Zentera. Quality signs are the usual ones for wool: dense, even knitting, clean seams, low pilling on a rub test, and shrink-resist information for machine-washable items.

Care in detail

ZQ does not change care; follow merino guidance and the label. Machine-washable merino (shrink-resist treated) can go on a wool or gentle cycle at 30 C or cooler, inside out, with a wool detergent free of protease enzymes and bleach. Untreated wool should be hand washed in lukewarm water with minimal agitation, since heat plus friction causes felting. Dry flat in the shade, reshaping while damp; do not tumble dry unless permitted. Steam or iron on the wool setting with a press cloth. De-pill with a comb. Store clean and dry with cedar or in sealed bags, as moth and carpet-beetle larvae eat keratin.

Care at a glance

Follow the finished product’s care label. A trademark does not specify the care needs of every blend or construction.

Environmental and social footprint

Wool's footprint is dominated by the farm: land use, enteric methane from sheep, fertilizer and pasture management, with scouring water and energy downstream. ZQ addresses farm practices through audited standards and ZQ+ through regenerative metrics, but no independent comparison of ZQ farms against conventional farms was found for this entry, so claimed benefits are program statements. Animal welfare is where the standard is most specific: it bans mulesing, the removal of skin strips from the breech to prevent flystrike. Peer-reviewed Australian research supports the idea that alternatives to surgical mulesing reduce harm; a 2012 study in the Australian Veterinary Journal (Hemsworth and colleagues) found behavioral effects on lambs of plastic breech clips were smaller than those of surgical mulesing. Flystrike control without mulesing still needs breeding and management.

Science

Protein-based natural material

Most hair is keratin; silk is a different protein-fiber system.

Structure and chemistry

ZQ does not change fiber structure. Merino wool is alpha-keratin, a protein whose helical chains are cross-linked by cystine disulfide bonds and organized into orthocortical and paracortical cells that produce natural crimp. The surface carries overlapping cuticle scales that cause directional friction and felting, and a thin covalently bound lipid layer makes the surface mildly water-repellent while the interior absorbs moisture. Fine merino is commonly in the high teens to low twenties of microns, but a program label does not specify micron.

From source to yarn

Wool under any sourcing program follows the usual route: shearing, skirting and classing on farm, baling, then scouring to remove grease, suint and dirt, followed by carding and combing into top for worsted yarns (or carding alone for the woollen route), spinning, and knitting or weaving. A sourcing program adds farm-level auditing, grower agreements and traceability documentation to these steps. The specific audit protocol, frequency and chain-of-custody rules should be taken from ZQ's current standard.

Performance in use

Performance is that of the wool specification, not the program. Merino has high moisture regain among common textile fibers, absorbing water vapor into the keratin with a heat of sorption; good elastic recovery from its crimp; charring and self-extinguishing flame behavior; and susceptibility to felting under heat, moisture and agitation unless shrink-resist treated. It dyes with acid, metal-complex and reactive dyes that bind to amino groups. Keratin is attacked by clothes moth and carpet beetle larvae, and prolonged UV exposure causes yellowing.

How it is identified

Burn tests (indicative only, and to be done with care) show wool burning slowly with a burnt-hair odor and crushable black ash. Microscopy shows cuticle scale patterns, and laboratory diameter measurement can confirm micron. No laboratory method proves that wool came from a ZQ farm; that depends on program documentation and certificates. A common confusion is treating ZQ as a fiber type or as a guarantee of a particular fineness.

History

Timeline

  1. 2007The New Zealand Merino Company, now called Zentera, began working with growers under the ZQ name. [1]

ZQ reflects the development of differentiated wool sourcing and grower relationships. Its name belongs in the provenance conversation alongside the underlying wool specification.

The deeper record

Wool sourcing programs grew as brands responded to concerns about animal welfare, particularly flystrike prevention and mulesing in merino flocks, and to demand for traceable, farm-level supply. ZQ emerged in this context from New Zealand merino supply chains, linking growers with apparel and outdoor brands through farm standards and supply agreements. Because the program's history, ownership and standard revisions could not be verified from primary sources in this pass, specific dates and organizational details are omitted rather than guessed.

Labeling and law

ZQ is a program trademark, not a generic fiber name, so labels must still state wool and its percentage. In the US, wool labeling is governed by the Wool Products Labeling Act of 1939 and FTC rules at 16 CFR Part 300; in the EU, Regulation (EU) No 1007/2011 requires the name wool for sheep's fleece. Buyers comparing ZQ with other schemes, such as Textile Exchange's Responsible Wool Standard, should compare the published standards, including each scheme's position on mulesing, and confirm certificate scope for the product.

Frequently asked

What does ZQ mean on a wool label?

It means the wool came from growers audited under the ZQ standard, run by Zentera (formerly The New Zealand Merino Company), covering animal welfare, environment and social responsibility. It does not describe the wool's fineness or quality.

Is ZQ merino mulesing-free?

Yes, according to the program, which states that mulesing is not permitted on properties supplying ZQ wool. Growers manage flystrike through other strategies. This is a program claim verified through its farm audits.

What is the difference between ZQ and ZQ+?

ZQ is the base audited farm standard. ZQ+, formerly called ZQRX, is a regenerative program that builds on it by measuring and working to improve farm outcomes such as soil and biodiversity over time. Both are run by Zentera.

Is ZQ the same as the Responsible Wool Standard?

No. The Responsible Wool Standard is a Textile Exchange standard; ZQ is a company-run program. Both prohibit mulesing, but they differ in governance and detail. Zentera also supplies RWS-certified wool alongside ZQ.
