Overview

Rabbit hair from breeds other than, or not identified as, Angora. It is not automatically equivalent to angora down.

Feel and behavior

Variable; often used in felt or blends.

Where you find it

Felt, hats, blends and specialty yarn.

Source and geography

Rabbit hair in textiles comes from the domestic rabbit (Oryctolagus cuniculus). The atlas separates it from angora: angora is the long wool of Angora breeds, harvested repeatedly from living animals and selected for fineness, whereas generic rabbit hair is typically the shorter fur of other breeds, often recovered from pelts and historically sold as fur for hat felt. Angora breeding research is active in China (for example the Wan and Zhexi strains) and has a history in France, while ordinary rabbit fur is a by-product of rabbit meat and pelt production.

Advantages

  • Rabbit fur felts readily into dense, smooth hat bodies.
  • Medullated hair is light and warm and adds a soft halo to blends.
  • Rabbit pelts and hair are largely by-products of rabbit meat production.
  • Fur felt hats cost less than beaver felt of similar appearance.

Drawbacks

  • Short, smooth fibers shed from knitwear and cling to other clothes.
  • Rabbit felt is less water resistant and shape-stable than beaver felt, according to hatters.
  • Hat X ratings are brand-specific marketing scales, not a standard.
  • Welfare of caged rabbits in meat and fur farming is a recognized concern.

Worth knowing

  • There is no industry-wide definition of the X rating printed inside many fur felt hats.
  • Rex rabbit fur quality is studied genetically, with markers associated with hair density identified in Rex populations.
  • European hare, a different genus from the domestic rabbit, is a traditional ingredient in fine hat felt.

Types, grades and fabrics

Types and grades

Generic rabbit hair divides into several trade forms. Rex rabbits are a fur breed whose short, plush coat is judged by hair density, length, fineness and hide area; research on Rex rabbits in China found winter-slaughtered pelts of better quality than summer ones. Meat-breed pelts supply lower-grade fur and loose hair. Hat felt uses rabbit fur, European hare fur (a different genus, Lepus) and beaver, alone or blended. Hatters' X ratings describe such blends, but there is no industry standard for what an X value means, so each brand's scale stands alone. Angora rabbit wool is a separate, long-haired product and is labeled differently in the EU.

Fabrics and products

Rabbit fur is felted into hat bodies for fedoras, trilbies and western hats, where it gives a smooth, dense surface. Loose rabbit hair is blended, usually as a minority share, with wool, nylon or other fibers in knitwear and yarns to add a soft halo. Dressed Rex and other rabbit pelts are used for fur trims, linings, throws and accessories; lapin is a French-derived trade term for rabbit fur in the fur trade. Short rabbit hair is also used in some flocked and felted craft products.

Buying and care

How to judge quality

For hats, fur felt should feel dense, smooth and springy, and hold its shape when the brim is flexed; hatters state that pure rabbit felt is less dense than beaver and more prone to losing shape in rain. Because X ratings are not standardized, compare fur content statements rather than X counts across brands. For knitwear, check the fiber label for a rabbit hair percentage and avoid confusing it with angora. Heavy shedding when the fabric is rubbed indicates loosely held short fibers. In the US, fur products must name the animal, so rabbit should be stated even when trim is marketed as lapin.

Care in detail

For fur felt hats, brush with a soft hat brush in the direction of the nap, handle by the brim rather than the crown, and store upside down on the crown or in a hat box away from heat. If a hat gets wet, shake off water and let it dry naturally at room temperature with its shape supported; never use direct heat. For rabbit-blend knitwear, hand wash cool with a wool detergent, avoid agitation, and dry flat; shedding reduces with wear. Fur trims should be professionally cleaned by a fur specialist. Keep all rabbit products away from moths and carpet beetles. The product label governs.

Care at a glance

Follow the complete product’s care label. Blends, dyes, finishes and construction can change suitable washing, drying and storage.

Environmental and social footprint

Most rabbit fur and hair is a by-product of meat production, with some farms breeding Rex rabbits specifically for pelts. Welfare concerns center on cage housing, space and slaughter, which are the subject of EU policy debate on caged farming. Pelt dressing uses tanning and finishing chemicals, and historical hat felting used mercury compounds, now replaced in regulated industries. No life cycle assessment specific to rabbit hair textiles was identified in this review. Fur-trim claims such as by-product or humane need documentation of the farm system, because the finished fiber reveals nothing about how the animal was kept.

Tradeoffs

Origin, processing inputs, labor, product construction and useful lifetime all need specific evidence. The name alone does not establish responsible production.

A useful question to ask: What exact material, product form and documented producer stand behind this name?

Science

Protein-based natural material

Most hair is keratin; silk is a different protein-fiber system.

Structure and chemistry

Rabbit coats contain fine underfur and coarser awn and guard hairs. Rabbit hair is keratin, and under the microscope many fibers show a medulla made of rows of air-filled cells, often described as ladder-like, which makes the fibers light and insulating but lowers bending strength. Angora selection favors fine, weakly medullated fibers: an INRA study of French Angora rabbits measured the proportion of medullated fibers at only about 0.1 to 7.3 percent and a mean comfort factor (share of fibers at or below 30 microns) of about 97.5 percent. Ordinary rabbit fur is shorter and has more guard hair, which suits felting better than spinning.

From source to yarn

For hat felt, fur is separated from cleaned pelts, treated to improve its felting capacity, formed into a loose hood and consolidated by heat, moisture, acid and mechanical working before blocking and finishing. Historically, mercury compounds were used in this treatment, a step known as carroting, and exposed hatters to mercury. Where rabbit hair is spun rather than felted, it is usually blended as a minority share with wool or nylon because the smooth, short fibers shed and lack cohesion. Angora wool, by contrast, is harvested by shearing, combing or plucking and can be spun at high percentages.

Performance in use

Rabbit hair is very light and warm because of its air-filled medulla, and it felts readily, which is why it was prized for dense, smooth hat felts. In knitted yarns it produces a halo but tends to shed and to mat with abrasion and moisture. It absorbs water vapor like other keratin fibers, chars rather than melts, and dyes with acid dyes, though highly medullated fibers can dye paler. Low strength per fiber and short length mean that blends rely on the carrier fiber for durability. It is susceptible to moths and to felting shrinkage in hot, agitated washing.

How it is identified

Burn testing (indicative only; do it with care) shows typical hair behavior and cannot separate rabbit from other keratin fibers. Microscopy is more useful: the multi-row medulla and fine scale pattern are characteristic, and the near absence of medullation in fine angora helps separate it from rabbit fur rich in guard hair. Optical diameter analyzers such as OFDA measure diameter and comfort factor, but the INRA study found OFDA is not adapted to measuring medulla opacity or size in angora without special calibration.

History

  3. 2011Regulation (EU) No 1007/2011 reserved the name angora for Angora rabbit hair, separating it from generic rabbit hair on EU labels. [2]

Rabbit hair has a long place in felt making. The distinction between a pelt, loose hair and a specialty down yarn is essential to understanding its history.

The deeper record

Rabbit fur was a major raw material of the felt hat trade. Its mercury hazard is well documented: Wedeen (1989) traces the first medical description of mercurialism in hatters to a report by J. A. Freeman in the Transactions of the Medical Society of New Jersey in 1860, and records that the tremor known as the hatters' shakes was rampant among immigrant workers. Medical findings did not end the practice; the occupational disease was curbed only in 1941, when mercury was required for detonators in World War II. Angora rabbit breeding for spinning wool developed separately and now includes genomic selection research.

Labeling and law

Under 16 CFR 303.6 the FTC permits the name of a fur-bearing animal with the word fiber, hair or blend when present at 5 percent or more, and the FTC's labeling guide gives Angora Rabbit Hair as an example of an acceptable disclosure; the term fur fiber may be used when no claim about the animal is made. In Regulation (EU) No 1007/2011, angora is reserved for hair of the angora rabbit, while other rabbit hair falls in the general animal hair category. Harvest method claims (shearing, combing or plucking) need documentation because the fiber does not reveal them.

This describes what rules and standards cover. It is not legal advice; jurisdiction, product form and current rule text control.

Frequently asked

Is rabbit hair the same as angora?

No. Angora is the long, fine wool of Angora rabbit breeds and, in EU labeling, the name angora is reserved for it. Generic rabbit hair is shorter fur from other breeds, often recovered from pelts, and is mainly used in felt and as a minority blend fiber.

What does 4X or 10X mean on a felt hat?

X ratings describe the fur blend and density of a felt hat, with higher numbers usually meaning more beaver or finer fur. There is no industry standard for the scale, so a rating is only meaningful within one maker's line.

Why does my rabbit-blend sweater shed?

Rabbit hairs are short and smooth, so they hold weakly in a yarn and work loose with friction. Shedding usually lessens after early wear. Washing gently and avoiding rubbing against dark, fuzzy fabrics helps.

Is rabbit fur felt better than wool felt?

Fur felt is generally finer, smoother, lighter and more durable in shape than wool felt, and costs more. Wool felt hats are cheaper and heavier. Beaver felt is typically the most weather resistant of the three.
