Overview

Wool recovered from production waste or used textiles, sorted and mechanically opened.

Feel and behavior

Varies with the input; often blended to balance shorter recovered fibers.

Where you find it

Coats, blankets, knitwear and felted products.

Source and geography

Recycled wool is keratin fiber recovered from existing wool products rather than shorn from sheep. Inputs range from clean pre-consumer waste, such as spinning waste, knitting offcuts and cutting-room clippings, to post-consumer garments sorted by fiber and color. In industry, mechanical fiber recycling is applied mostly to natural-polymer fibers, including wool and cashmere, and to cotton and man-made cellulosics; one Spanish spinner, Hilaturas Ferre, operates plants for recycling post-industrial wool and polyester. Because inputs vary, recycled wool is often blended with virgin fibers to restore length and strength.

Advantages

  • It keeps wool's warmth, moisture absorption and resilience.
  • It reuses fiber that would otherwise be discarded, avoiding new sheep farming for that share.
  • Color-sorted inputs can avoid redyeing, saving water, chemicals and energy.
  • Dense recycled wool cloth works well for coats, blankets and felts.

Drawbacks

  • Shorter fibers make yarns weaker and more prone to pilling than new wool.
  • Synthetic fibers are often blended in, making later recycling harder.
  • Mixed inputs can carry residues of previous dyes, finishes or stray fibers.
  • Recycled status cannot be verified in the finished fabric, so claims rely on certificates.

Worth knowing

  • Prato in Tuscany has recycled wool since the mid-nineteenth century, and its rag sorters, the cenciaioli, were known for identifying fibers by touch.
  • The old trade names shoddy and mungo distinguished longer fiber torn from soft goods from shorter fiber torn from hard-milled cloth.

Types, grades and fabrics

Types and grades

Recycled wool is classed by input. Pre-consumer material, such as spinning waste, knitting offcuts and cutting-room clippings, is clean and color-sorted; post-consumer material comes from worn garments sorted by hand for fiber and color. The older trade distinguished shoddy, torn from soft knitted or loosely woven goods and relatively long, from mungo, torn from hard-milled woven or felted cloth and shorter. Recovered fiber is usually blended with virgin wool for length or with polyamide or polyester for strength and spinnability. Certified grades follow Textile Exchange standards: the Recycled Claim Standard (RCS) applies from 5% recycled input, and the Global Recycled Standard (GRS) from 20%, with consumer-facing GRS labels requiring at least 50%.

Fabrics and products

Because the fibers are short, recycled wool is mostly carded and woollen spun rather than combed. It is woven into dense, fulled coatings such as melton and overcoat cloth, tweeds and flannels, and into blankets and throws. Knitters use it for chunky sweaters, beanies and scarves, often in heathered colors from mixed-shade fiber. Prato in Tuscany is known for regenerated carded wool cloth for coats and jackets. Recycled wool also goes into felts, upholstery fabrics, needle-punched nonwovens, insulation batts and padding, where length matters less than bulk and resilience.

Buying and care

How to judge quality

Read the percentages: 'recycled wool' may be a minority share, blended with virgin wool, polyamide or polyester, and each must be listed by weight on US labels. Hairier, slightly harsher cloth and speckled or heathered color are normal. Pilling is the main durability risk, so choose dense, fulled weaves over loose knits for hard-wearing garments. Rub the surface: excessive loose fibers or dust point to short, over-processed fiber. Ask for a GRS or RCS certificate covering the product, not just the mill, and remember that no lab test can prove wool was recycled. Prices can be similar to virgin wool when inputs are carefully sorted.

Care in detail

Care for recycled wool as for other wool, following the label for any blend. Air garments between wears, brush coats with a clothes brush, and spot clean small marks. When washing is allowed, use a wool or hand-wash cycle in cold or lukewarm water, about 30 C at most, with a wool detergent free of protease enzymes, and avoid agitation, which causes felting and shrinkage. Press water out in a towel and dry flat away from direct heat. Steam or iron at the wool setting, about 150 C (two dots), through a damp cloth. Remove pills with a comb or shaver. Store clean in breathable bags, with cedar or regular inspection against clothes moths and carpet beetles.

Care at a glance

Care for the whole blend according to the label. Air between wears and repair small damage early.

Environmental and social footprint

The main benefit of recycled wool is avoided virgin fiber: new wool carries land use, methane from sheep and scouring effluent, while mechanical recycling mainly needs sorting labor and electricity for tearing and carding. Where inputs are sorted by color, redyeing can be skipped. Prato has recycled wool since the mid-nineteenth century, with rag sorters called cenciaioli who could identify fibers by touch; the district is now the subject of academic case studies of circular textile networks. Independent, dated life cycle figures specific to recycled wool yarn are scarce, so percentage savings in marketing should be treated as producer claims. Blending with synthetics adds persistent microfibers and complicates end-of-life recycling.

Tradeoffs

Recovered fibers can be shorter. Input quality, blend share and new dyeing determine the result.

A useful question to ask: Is the input pre-consumer or post-consumer, and what percentage is recycled?

Science

Recovered material, retained chemistry

Recycled describes the feedstock history, not a new polymer family.

Structure and chemistry

Chemically the fiber is unchanged wool keratin: alpha-helical keratin filaments in a sulfur-rich matrix cross-linked by disulfide bonds, wrapped in overlapping cuticle scales. Research on separating wool from nylon carpet waste has shown that gentle processing can preserve wool's scale structure and tensile strength close to those of virgin fiber. What changes is the physical profile. Tearing and garneting break fibers, so recycled wool has a shorter, more variable length distribution, more broken ends and some dust, and it keeps the dyes, finishes and treatments of its previous life. Mixed inputs bring residues of other fibers, such as polyester or elastane, into the new yarn.

From source to yarn

Collected textiles are sorted by fiber content and color, often manually and with growing use of near-infrared scanning; buttons, zips, labels and other hard points are removed, and some lines cut waste into clippings and eject pieces with trims using cameras. The material is opened by pickers and pulled apart on a Garnett or tearing line into loose fiber. Lubricants applied before shredding can reduce fiber damage. Because color is retained, careful shade sorting can avoid redyeing. The fiber is blended, often with virgin wool or synthetic staple, carded and respun into yarn, or processed into felts and nonwovens.

Performance in use

Recycled wool keeps wool's moisture absorption, warmth and resilience, but shorter fibers give more hairiness, lower yarn strength and more pilling unless the fiber is blended or densely constructed, which suits it to coats, blankets, felts and heavier knitwear. Because it retains its scales, it can still felt and shrink in hot, agitated washing unless an earlier shrink-resist finish survives. Quality depends on the input: clean, sorted pre-consumer waste gives better fiber than mixed post-consumer streams, and mechanical recycling cannot be repeated indefinitely because each pass shortens the fibers further.

How it is identified

Burn tests are indicative only; recycled wool burns like other wool, smoldering with a burning-hair smell and leaving crushable dark ash. Microscopy shows ordinary wool scale patterns, often with more broken ends, short fibers and mixed colors within one yarn, and may reveal stray synthetic or cellulosic fibers from mixed inputs. No routine laboratory test proves that a wool fiber was recycled, so claims rest on purchase and production records and transaction certificates. FTIR or solubility tests can quantify contaminating fibers in the blend.

Recovering wool from existing cloth is older than contemporary circular-fashion language. Mechanical opening and color sorting allow another textile use, often with blending to address shorter fibers. The input history should remain visible.

The deeper record

Reclaiming wool is far older than circular-fashion language. The nineteenth-century trade in shoddy, fiber torn from wool rags, supplied cheap cloth, and the US Wool Products Labeling Act of 1939 reflected the trade's concern to separate reclaimed wool from new fleece so buyers could tell them apart. The statutory recycled category still groups unused manufacturing waste with worn goods, unlike the pre-consumer and post-consumer vocabulary of voluntary standards. Current research adds chemical and biological separation: keratinase enzymes can digest the wool out of wool-polyester blends to recover clean polyester, and mild acids can dissolve nylon 6 out of wool carpet waste.

Labeling and law

Under the Wool Products Labeling Act of 1939 (15 U.S.C. 68 et seq.) and 16 CFR Part 300, US labels must disclose wool and recycled wool separately by percentage of weight. The Act defines recycled wool as fiber returned to a fibrous state from wool that was woven or felted into a product never used by a consumer, or from wool products a consumer did use. Descriptors implying newness, such as virgin wool, are reserved for wool never reclaimed. EU labels follow Regulation (EU) No 1007/2011, and recycled content is commonly certified under the Global Recycled Standard or Recycled Claim Standard.

This describes what rules and standards cover. It is not legal advice; jurisdiction, product form and current rule text control.

Frequently asked

Is recycled wool as good as new wool?

It is chemically the same wool, so it keeps warmth and moisture handling, but its fibers are shorter. That usually means more pilling and less strength, which is why it is often blended and used in dense coats, blankets and chunky knits rather than fine suiting.

How is wool recycled?

Garments and waste are sorted by fiber and color, stripped of buttons and zips, then torn and garnetted back into loose fiber. The fiber is blended, carded and spun into new woollen yarn or made into felts and nonwovens. Chemical recycling of wool is still at the research stage.

What do GRS and RCS mean on recycled wool?

They are Textile Exchange chain-of-custody standards. The Recycled Claim Standard verifies recycled content from 5% of input. The Global Recycled Standard applies from 20% recycled content, adds social, environmental and chemical requirements, and allows a consumer-facing product label only when the product contains at least 50% recycled material.

Why is recycled wool often blended with nylon or polyester?

Tearing shortens wool fibers, so spinners add longer, stronger synthetic fibers to hold the yarn together and reduce breakage and pilling. The trade-off is that a mixed-fiber product is harder to recycle again and sheds synthetic microfibers.
