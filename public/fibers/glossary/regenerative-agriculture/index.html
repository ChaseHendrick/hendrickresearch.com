Regenerative agriculture

Also searched as: regenerative farming, regenerative, regenerative grazing, regenerative organic, regenerative cotton, regenerative wool

A family of farming and grazing approaches aimed at rebuilding soil health, biodiversity and ecosystem processes, which still lacks a consensus definition or a single standardized certification.

In detail

Regenerative agriculture is usually defined in one of two ways: by practices, such as reduced tillage, cover crops, diverse rotations, organic amendments and integrated livestock or managed grazing, or by outcomes, such as rising soil organic matter, better nutrient and water cycling and greater biodiversity. Fiber claims mostly involve cotton, wool and other animal fibers from farms in such programs. Unlike organic agriculture, which regulated certification defines largely by permitted inputs, regenerative claims mean whatever the program or standard behind them specifies.

The science and numbers

Definitions in the literature fall into practice-based and outcome-based families, and researchers have identified several distinct discourses behind them. A 2025 proposal instead frames regenerative agriculture as an ecological ethic and approach that supports ecosystem processes, arguing that whether a site is regenerating depends on site-specific measurement of its trajectory and rate of change over time. A 2025 review estimated adoption at under 1% of global farmed acreage and named an unresolved operational definition and the lack of standardized certification as barriers. Measuring soil carbon change requires sampling to a stated depth, bulk density data and repeat sampling at the same sites, because changes are small relative to existing stocks and vary across a field.

A practical example

Two wool suppliers both describe their farms as regenerative. One cites enrollment in a program that monitors soil and vegetation indicators at fixed sites over years; the other cites a set of adopted grazing practices without measurements. A student should ask which program applies, what is verified and by whom, and whether the fiber delivered is traceable to those farms.

What to distinguish

Regenerative is not a synonym for organic, carbon neutral or certified; a regenerative claim can exist with or without any of these. Soil carbon gains are slow, variable and reversible, so sequestration claims deserve particular scrutiny. Scholars note that a definition based on a practice list is easier to certify but also easier to use as a marketing label. Without a named standard and verification, the word alone conveys little that can be checked.

Origins and history

One 2025 review describes the concept as formalized more than 40 years ago while drawing on much older Indigenous land-care philosophies. Early academic definitions tied it to rising soil biological productivity, low external inputs and organic farming. Calls for regenerative agriculture have increased sharply in recent years, including in food and fiber supply chains, which is why scholars now debate whether it should be defined by practices, by outcomes or by an underlying ethic.
