Ginning

Also searched as: cotton gin, lint, seed cotton

Separating cotton lint from seeds and other material.

In detail

Ginning separates cotton lint from seeds after harvesting. Cleaning operations remove additional unwanted material, and the prepared lint is compressed into bales for transport and later textile production. This is an early agricultural-processing stage, before a spinning mill turns the fiber into yarn.

The science and numbers

Two mechanisms dominate. In a saw gin, rotating toothed saws catch the lint and draw it through gaps between ribs that are too narrow for the seed to pass, after which the fibers are removed from the teeth. In a roller gin, lint clings to a moving roller and is pulled past a close-fitting knife while a rotating bar or knife strips the seed away. Roller ginning is the gentler process: it leaves fewer fiber entanglements (neps) and preserves the length of extra-long-staple cottons such as Pima, whose fine fibers saw teeth tend to break and tangle. Saw gins descend from machines designed for short-staple upland cotton. Recent economic work in the United States compares the relative cost of ginning with saw, conventional roller and high-speed roller gins, so the choice of gin is both a fiber-quality and a cost decision.

A practical example

In a cotton supply-chain record, distinguish seed cotton arriving at a gin from a lint bale leaving it. Those are different material forms. Keeping the stage clear makes it easier to interpret quantities and to understand why incoming and outgoing masses are not interchangeable.

What to distinguish

Ginning does not mean spinning, combing or fabric washing. Nor does the gin’s location automatically identify the farm for every bale. An origin claim needs records that connect the relevant stages.

Origins and history

Gin is a shortening of 'engine'. Eli Whitney applied for a patent on his cotton gin on October 28, 1793, and it was granted on March 14, 1794; the National Archives holds the patent drawing. His machine used a toothed wheel to pull fibers through a fine metal mesh, leaving the seeds behind, and was designed for short-staple cotton; a single device could clean up to about fifty pounds of cotton a day. The expansion of cotton growing that followed created a vast new market for enslaved labor in the United States.
