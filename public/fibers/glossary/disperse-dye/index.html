Disperse dye

Also searched as: disperse dyes, disperse dyestuff, polyester dye, HT dyeing, high-temperature dyeing, Disperse Blue, disperse azo dye

A disperse dye is a small, nonionic, sparingly water-soluble colorant applied as a fine aqueous dispersion that dissolves into hydrophobic fibers, chiefly polyester.

In detail

Polyester has no ionic sites and is too hydrophobic for water-soluble dyes. Disperse dyes are milled into fine particles and held in suspension by dispersing agents; a small fraction dissolves in the bath, diffuses into the fiber's amorphous regions and remains there as a solid solution. Uptake is slow at the boil because polyester chains are tightly packed, so polyester is usually dyed in pressurized machines above 100 C. Disperse dyes also color nylon, acetate and similar hydrophobic fibers.

The science and numbers

Dyeing proceeds by dissolution of dye molecules from particles, adsorption at the fiber surface and diffusion into the polymer, the rate-limiting step. Diffusion rises sharply once the polymer passes its glass transition and segmental motion opens free volume, which is why temperature dominates. Studies synthesizing new azo disperse dyes report clear gains in color strength when dyeing temperature rises from 100 C to 130 C, and high-temperature, high-pressure dyeing giving the highest K/S values compared with other methods. Fastness is assessed for washing, rubbing, light and sublimation using grey scale ratings. Chemically, many disperse dyes are azo or anthraquinone structures with polar substituents that tune shade and affinity. Patch-test studies list Disperse Blue 106 and 124 among allergens tested.

A practical example

A lab dyes the same polyester fabric twice with one disperse dye, once at 100 C and once at 130 C in a pressurized vessel. The 130 C sample is noticeably deeper. After a hot-press test, the lighter sample shows little change, but a deep navy sample may mark an adjacent white fabric, showing why sublimation fastness is checked for polyester.

What to distinguish

Disperse dyes cannot color cotton meaningfully, so polyester-cotton blends need a second dye class for the cotton. Some disperse dyes are recognized contact allergens, and textile dye mixes appear in dermatological patch testing. Deep shades can migrate or sublime during heat setting, pressing or storage. Good wash results do not guarantee good sublimation or light fastness.

Origins and history

Disperse dyes were developed for hydrophobic manufactured fibers that ionic dyes colored poorly, and their importance grew with polyester, which now accounts for a large share of fiber production. Modern research continues to design new disperse structures, dispersants and waterless or low-water application routes, including digital inkjet systems for polyester.
