Dehairing

Also searched as: dehaired, undercoat separation

Separating fine undercoat from coarser guard hair.

In detail

Dehairing separates the fine undercoat sought for textiles from the coarser outer hairs present in some animal fleeces. Cashmere is associated with the fine, dehaired undercoat of the cashmere goat. Separation matters because an average description of a fleece can conceal very different fiber populations.

The science and numbers

Dehairing works because down and guard hair differ in diameter, stiffness, length and mass. In one study of Changthangi goats, down averaged about 13 microns and 48 mm long while guard hair averaged about 68 microns and 81 mm; in coarser Bakerwal and Kargil goats guard hair exceeded 100 microns. Fleece composition varies too: Raeini cashmere goats in Iran averaged about 66 percent cashmere and 34 percent guard hair. Machines exploit these contrasts with repeated opening and carding-type actions in which heavier, stiffer hairs are thrown out while fine down is carried forward, and several passes may be needed. The US statutory test shows what counts as success: a cashmere product must have a mean diameter not over 19 microns, a coefficient of variation not over 24 percent and no more than 3 percent by weight of fibers over 30 microns.

A practical example

When discussing a cashmere lot, ask whether measurements describe the raw mixed fleece or the prepared textile fiber. A soft-looking photograph cannot establish how effectively coarse hairs were removed. Composition and diameter information provide different pieces of evidence.

What to distinguish

Dehairing is not simply another name for washing, nor does it mean that every remaining fiber has an identical diameter. Avoid judging finished comfort from a process name alone; yarn construction and surface finishing also matter.

Origins and history

Kashmir shawls woven from fine goat down were luxury goods in Mughal and later courts, and nineteenth-century European mills copied their buta motif so extensively that it took the name of Paisley. European industry then shifted from importing finished shawls to importing raw down, and Bradford firms such as Joseph Dawson Ltd of Cashmere Works industrialized mechanical dehairing in the late nineteenth century. A 1996 review noted that the absence of acceptable small-scale dehairing equipment limited cottage-scale cashmere processing in the United States.
