Fabric construction

Weaving

Also searched as: woven fabric, loom, interlacing

Interlacing yarn systems to create fabric.

In detail

Weaving constructs fabric by interlacing yarn systems. In a conventional loom, warp yarns are arranged lengthwise and weft is inserted across them. Different interlacing sequences produce structures such as plain weave, twill and satin; those structures are not themselves fiber types.

The science and numbers

A loom repeats three primary motions: shedding, which lifts selected warp ends to open a gap; picking, which inserts weft through the shed; and beating-up, in which the reed pushes the new pick against the cloth, while let-off and take-up keep warp tension and advance the fabric. The interlacing plan sets the float length. Plain weave, written 1/1, has the shortest floats and highest crimp; twills need at least three sheds and satins at least five. Experimental comparisons of plain, 2/2 twill and eight-end satin fabrics woven from the same yarns report measurable differences in stiffness and tensile behavior, with float length identified as a controlling factor. Compactness has environmental effects as well: in one laundering study, woven polyester shed about 17 fibers per square centimeter against about 24 for a knit, and alkali treatment cut woven shedding by up to 89.6 percent.

A practical example

Two fabrics can both be cotton while one uses a plain weave and the other a twill. A composition label identifies the material, while a construction description helps explain its surface and behavior. Both are needed to describe the textile usefully.

What to distinguish

Woven does not automatically mean rigid, waterproof or non-stretch. Yarn selection, interlacing, density and finishing influence the final performance. Compare finished samples for the use you have in mind.

Origins and history

Weave complexity tracks loom capability. Plain weave needs only two alternating sheds, so it can be produced on the simplest looms, which explains why it underlies many unrelated weaving traditions and so many fabric names distinguished by fiber and finish, from calico to taffeta. Twills required a third shaft, and satins demanded more shafts or a figure-harness mechanism; the extra control produced families of named fabrics such as drill, serge, gabardine and denim.
