Cottonization

Also searched as: cottonisation, cottonized hemp, cottonised flax

Processing a bast fiber into shorter, finer elements suited to cotton-type equipment.

In detail

Cottonization adapts bast fibers to shorter-staple processing systems. The route can involve opening, shortening and refining fiber bundles, using mechanical and sometimes wet-processing steps. Its purpose is compatibility with equipment and blends, rather than changing the plant species or turning hemp or flax into cotton.

The science and numbers

Bast technical fibers are bundles of elementary cells bound by pectin and hemicellulose, and long hemp bast is reported at about 55 to 72 percent cellulose with about 2 to 5 percent lignin. Cottonization breaks those bundles into finer units, removes residual woody shive and cuts the fiber to lengths that short-staple cotton machinery can draft, using mechanical opening and, in some routes, chemical or enzymatic degumming. The target is set by cotton itself: upland cotton staple typically runs about 7/8 to 1 5/16 inches. The alternative is the long-fiber route, and a 2018 study that scutched and hackled hemp on flax machinery found yellow-stem varieties scutched more efficiently and controlled dew retting gave a higher yield of hackled fiber than bio-degumming. In cottonized blends the hemp share is hard to confirm by eye, so percentage claims depend on documentation and quantitative analysis.

A practical example

A spinner might blend cottonized hemp with cotton to use an established short-staple production line. The resulting yarn still contains hemp. The blend ratio, remaining bundle size and processing conditions help determine its suitability for the planned fabric.

What to distinguish

Cottonized is a process description, not a fiber-content claim. It also differs from regenerated cellulose, where cellulose is dissolved and formed into a new fiber. Ask for the actual composition and preparation route.

Origins and history

The word describes a purpose: making bast fiber behave enough like cotton to run on the cotton spinning system. Interest revived as hemp regained legal status. In the United States, the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018 defines hemp as Cannabis sativa L. with not more than 0.3 percent delta-9 tetrahydrocannabinol on a dry weight basis (7 U.S.C. 1639o), and since 1 January 2023 the EU farm framework supports approved varieties up to 0.3 percent. Growth in cultivation brought renewed demand for decortication and cottonizing lines.
