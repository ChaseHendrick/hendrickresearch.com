Claims & sourcing

Post-consumer

Also searched as: post-consumer recycled, PCR

Material recovered after a product has been used by its end user.

In detail

Post-consumer material comes from products that have reached an end user and are recovered after their intended use. In a recycled-content claim, the term describes the source stage of the input. It does not by itself specify whether the input was a textile, packaging or another product.

The science and numbers

Post-consumer feedstock is heterogeneous, and its composition limits what recycling can do. A study of 2,173 kg of textiles (9,333 items) donated to Canadian thrift stores found about 55 percent suitable for immediate resale and 24 percent needing repair or cleaning; cotton was the dominant fiber at 50.2 percent of items and polyester followed at 29.2 percent, with many blends containing small amounts of spandex that can contaminate recycling streams. A Norwegian dataset documents 16,464 garments (4,157 kg) to support such estimates. Sorting increasingly relies on near-infrared spectroscopy with machine-learning classifiers to separate cotton, nylon and polyester. Recovery then diverges: mechanical opening shortens fibers, while polymer or pulp routes such as recycled cotton pulp for lyocell or viscose rebuild material. Bottle-derived polyester is also post-consumer, but it recycles packaging rather than closing a textile loop.

A practical example

A used garment collected for fiber recovery and a used plastic bottle processed into a textile feedstock have different original forms. Ask which input a claim refers to, what proportion of the final material it represents, and how that proportion is documented.

What to distinguish

Recycled input does not automatically mean the finished product can be recycled again in your local system. The applicable certification definition and evidence should accompany a specific commercial claim.

Origins and history

Distinguishing used from unused recycled material has a long legal history in wool. The US Wool Products Labeling Act of 1939 grouped reclaimed fiber into one 'recycled wool' category covering both unused manufactured products and products a consumer had used, reflecting a trade concern with shoddy. Voluntary standards and the FTC Green Guides later framed recycled-content claims around recovery from the waste stream. In the EU, the Waste Framework Directive requires separate collection of textile waste from 2025, which is driving the new data on post-consumer composition.
