Claims & sourcing

Traceability

Also searched as: provenance, origin records, supply-chain tracking

The ability to follow material history through specified records and stages.

In detail

Traceability is the ability to follow specified information about a material through recorded stages. A useful claim identifies what is traced, how far back the records reach and which links are supported. A map of plausible locations is not, by itself, a record for a specific product.

The science and numbers

Traceability can rest on documents, on markers added to the product, or on properties measured in the material itself. Documentary systems link purchases, batches and transaction certificates. Added markers include producer tracers, such as the FiberPrint additive that Unifi states lets its REPREVE content be detected later. Intrinsic fingerprints are an active research area: a 2026 study measured 21 mineral elements by ICP-MS in 237 cashmere samples from three regions of Inner Mongolia (Ordos, Chifeng and Alxa League) and four breeds, and identified six elements (magnesium, manganese, arsenic, strontium, cerium and uranium) that discriminated origin, with regional classification more accurate than breed. Species identity is a separate question answered by microscopy, DNA or proteomics. Some claims cannot be tested in the fiber at all: recycled and virgin PET, or lyocell from recycled and wood pulp, are chemically identical, so origin depends on records.

A practical example

For a garment, you might know the sewing factory but not the spinning mill or fiber origin. State that boundary clearly. To investigate further, ask for links between the product, batch records, purchases and the relevant upstream facilities.

What to distinguish

Traceability and certification are different ideas. A traceable material is not automatically certified to an environmental standard, and a general supplier certificate does not answer every product-origin question. Fibers of Earth routes remain illustrative.

Origins and history

Labeling law reaches only part of the chain. The US Textile Fiber Products Identification Act, approved on 2 September 1958, requires the generic fiber names and the country where the product was processed or manufactured, not where the fiber was grown. Traceability as a sourcing discipline grew beyond that baseline as buyers asked for farm, gin, mill and dyehouse links, and as certification schemes added shipment-level records. Claims such as Pima, Egyptian or organic cannot be confirmed by routine fiber tests and depend on documentation.
