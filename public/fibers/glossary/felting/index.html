Felting

Also searched as: felt, wet felting, needle felting, felted wool, felting shrinkage, directional frictional effect, DFE

Felting is the irreversible matting of wool and other animal fibers into a dense, entangled mass under moisture, warmth and agitation, used to make felt directly from loose fiber.

In detail

Wet felting starts from layers of loose fiber, usually laid in crossing directions. The fiber is wetted with warm soapy water and rubbed, rolled or pressed. Individual fibers creep toward their root ends, work between their neighbors and lock together; as agitation continues, the mass shrinks and firms into a coherent fabric with no yarns at all. Needle felting achieves entanglement dry, with barbed needles, and many industrial 'felts' are synthetic nonwovens made by needlepunching rather than true felting.

The science and numbers

The accepted mechanism is the directional frictional effect: friction is lower when a fiber slides root first, with the scales, than tip first, against them, because the scale edges point toward the tip. Cyclic compression and release during agitation therefore produces net migration of each fiber toward its root, a ratchet action that tangles the mass. Water and warmth soften the fiber and increase its extensibility and recovery, and alkaline or acid conditions intensify the effect, while crimp and elastic recovery pull the entangled mass tighter. Because scales drive the effect, chlorination and polymer coatings that smooth or mask them are the basis of shrink-resist finishing.

A practical example

A hat maker lays two thin, crossing layers of merino fiber, wets them with warm soapy water and rolls the bundle in a mat. At first fibers lift off easily; after several rounds of rolling the layer holds together as one piece and has shrunk visibly. Further throwing and kneading, which is fulling, produces a firm felt that can be shaped over a block.

What to distinguish

Felting ability varies: wool with pronounced scales and crimp felts readily, some hair fibers felt poorly, and plant and synthetic fibers do not wet felt at all. Superwash and other shrink-resist treatments are designed to stop felting. Retail 'felt' may be a needlepunched synthetic nonwoven with no scale-driven entanglement and quite different durability.

Origins and history

Wet felting needs no spinning or weaving, only fiber, water, soap and agitation, so it appears in many craft traditions and was long important for rugs, tents and garments among herding peoples. In Europe and North America it underpinned the hat trade. Fur for hat felts was treated with mercury compounds, a step called carroting, and a New Jersey physician described mercurialism in hatters in 1860. A historical review found the hazard was curbed only in 1941, when mercury was diverted to wartime detonators, and argued that the 'mad hatter' image owed more to popular perception than to medical findings.
