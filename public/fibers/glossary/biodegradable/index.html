Biodegradable

Also searched as: biodegradability, biodegradation, degradable, biodegradable fiber, biodegradable claim

Able to be broken down by microorganisms into carbon dioxide, water, methane and biomass; as a marketing claim, it needs evidence tied to realistic disposal conditions.

In detail

Biodegradation is a biological process. Microbes secrete enzymes that cut polymer chains into fragments small enough to absorb and metabolize, ultimately converting the carbon to carbon dioxide with oxygen present, or to methane and carbon dioxide without it. Cellulose and protein fibers are generally susceptible because common microbial enzymes, such as cellulases and proteases, attack their bonds, while most petroleum-based synthetic fibers degrade very slowly. Rates depend on temperature, moisture, oxygen, the microbial community, fiber crystallinity, finishes and dyes. In marketing, 'biodegradable' is an environmental claim that must be substantiated for the conditions consumers would reasonably expect.

The science and numbers

Laboratory evidence usually comes from respirometric tests, which measure carbon dioxide released, or oxygen consumed, by a sample held in soil, compost, sludge or seawater, and report it as a percentage of the theoretical maximum calculated from the sample's carbon content. Soil-burial tests for textiles instead track loss of tensile strength or mass, which shows deterioration but not complete mineralization. In the United States, 16 CFR 260.8 calls for competent and reliable scientific evidence that the entire item will completely break down and return to nature within a reasonably short time after customary disposal, with one year as the limit for items usually sent to landfills, incinerators or recycling facilities. In the EU, Directive (EU) 2024/825 restricts generic environmental claims from 27 September 2026.

A practical example

Two socks are advertised as 'biodegradable': one is untreated wool, the other a polyamide containing a degradation-promoting additive. A student would ask, for each, which test method was used, under what conditions and for how long, and what percentage of the material's carbon was converted. Then the key question: do those conditions resemble where used socks actually end up? The same word can rest on very different evidence.

What to distinguish

Biodegradable is not the same as compostable, which refers to a managed system with defined criteria, or bio-based, which describes where a feedstock came from. Fragmentation into smaller pieces is not biodegradation unless microbes also consume the fragments. Blends, elastane, coatings and synthetic sewing thread can stop a whole garment from biodegrading even when its main fiber would.

Origins and history

The word joins the prefix bio-, from Greek bios (life), to degradable. As the term spread through consumer marketing, regulators addressed it directly: the FTC's Green Guides, first issued in 1992, include a section on degradable claims, and the current 2012 text sets the one-year benchmark for landfill-bound items. In the EU, Directive (EU) 2024/825, adopted on 28 February 2024, amended consumer law to target unsubstantiated generic environmental claims.
