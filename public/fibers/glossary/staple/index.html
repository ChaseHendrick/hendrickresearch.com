Staple

Also searched as: staple fiber, staple fibre, cut staple, staple length

A fiber of finite length, which can be natural or manufactured.

In detail

Staple fibers have finite lengths and are assembled into yarn through overlap and an appropriate spinning process. Cotton and wool are familiar natural examples; manufactured fibers can also be cut to staple lengths. The length distribution matters, not just the longest fiber in a sample.

The science and numbers

Staple length is a distribution. For cotton, the High Volume Instrument measures bundles and reports upper half mean length, uniformity index and short fiber content, while AFIS measures single fibers and adds neps per gram and maturity. In a genome-wide study of 348 US upland cotton lines grown in 10 environments, length, strength and uniformity were positively correlated with one another and negatively with short fiber content, and all showed broad-sense heritability above 60 percent. USDA classing describes upland cotton at about 7/8 to 1 5/16 inches and American Pima at about 1 1/4 to 1 9/16 inches. Length matters because staple yarn holds together only through twist-generated friction, and drafting nips must be set longer than the longest fibers; short fibers float between nips and create unevenness. Manufactured staple is cut from filament tow and usually crimped for cohesion, which ASTM D3937 measures as crimp frequency.

A practical example

A polyester textile can use continuous-filament yarn or spun yarn made from cut polyester staple. The polymer name can therefore remain the same while the yarn construction changes. Ask for the fiber form when a composition label does not explain the texture you observe.

What to distinguish

Staple is not a quality rating or a synonym for natural fiber. “Long staple” needs context: relevant lengths and processing expectations differ between cotton, wool and other fiber systems.

Origins and history

In the wool trade a staple is a naturally formed lock of fibers in the fleece, and classers long judged fleeces by counting crimp waves along it. In cotton the word became a measured grade: USDA classification reports staple length in fractions of an inch, so a cotton's staple is a measured value for a specific lot, not a fixed property of its name. For manufactured fibers, staple simply denotes filament deliberately cut to a finite length so it can be processed on natural-fiber spinning systems.
